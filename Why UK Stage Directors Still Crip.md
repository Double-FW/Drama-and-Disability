# Why UK Stage Directors Still Crip-up Disability

## A Quiet Crisis Under the Spotlight

In the past few years, UK theatre has splashed headlines with landmark moments in disability representation: Deaf actresses commanding Shakespeare, sensory-accessible productions for neurodiverse audiences, disabled ensembles leading avant-garde works. Yet this progress cloaks a troubling persistence: the **continuing prevalence of "**[**cripping-up**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cripping-up)**",** non-disabled actors playing disability-coded roles, _and_ the near complete absence of incidental casting of disabled performers in roles not defined by their impairment.  
2024 and 2025 in English theatres there were multiple instances of cripping-up in productions of; _Oedipus, Richard III, Richard My Richard, The Glass Menagerie, Coram Boy, Dr Strangelove, The Human Body, Taming of the Shrew and Waiting for Godot._

## When Disability Becomes a Costume

Consider a high-profile cripping-up case:

In 2024, **Michelle Terry cast herself as Richard III** — a well know disabled character based on a disabled historical figure, the the first ever play written with ableism as a core theme, in a Globe Theatre production that omitted the portrayal of Richard's physical impairment entirely. This sparked widespread criticism from disabled artists and advocacy groups. One letter protested, highlighting that:

_"disability means exclusion, ostracisation… It isn't something one can wear for the sake of a show, and remove in the dressing room."_ [Disability News Service](https://www.disabilitynewsservice.com/outrage-at-globes-decision-to-cast-non-disabled-actor-as-richard-iii/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

**Jamie Hale**, a disabled director, lamented:

"I was profoundly disappointed… Richard III is one of the few disabled roles in the theatrical canon… To see this role given to a non-disabled actor represents the exclusion of disabled people even from those roles that explore the interiority of our lived experience." [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/stage/2024/feb/05/acting-disability-and-the-problem-with-lived-experience?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Even though **over 300 disabled performers, creative professionals and theatre companies signed the open letter**demanding recasting, Michelle Terry defended the decision, dismissing the backlash as rooted in misogyny:

_"The misogyny has far outweighed the disability discourse."_ [_The Times_](https://www.thetimes.co.uk/article/anger-over-richard-iii-casting-is-misogyny-says-michelle-terry-z6q9m7wh7?utm_source=chatgpt.com) _and_ [_The Guardian_](https://www.theguardian.com/stage/article/2024/may/21/the-level-of-hate-was-dangerous-michelle-terry-on-the-backlash-to-her-casting-as-richard-iii?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Although this was never evident either in on social media or in the dialogue with disabled professionals.

Despite this the theatre industry is reluctant to change. the Old Vic in 2025, Cecilia Noble — a non-disabled actor — played Tiresias, the blind prophet in _Oedipus,_ in 2024 revivals of Beckett's _Waiting for Godot_, both Pozzo (who becomes blind) and Lucky (whose muteness and physical subjugation are crucial to the role) were performed by non-disabled actors, and Shakespeare North also cripped-up Richard III in a production of Richard, My Richard, casting the non-disabled actor Kyle Rowe in the lead role.

## Why Directors Are Still Afraid of Disability

Industry commentary reveals that fear is a bigger barrier than logistical challenges. The consensus persists that directing a disabled actor in a non-disabled role risks authenticity — and invites discomfort. But as **Mat Fraser**, a disabled performer who triumphed in _American Horror Story_, points out:

_"The major barrier… is this accumulated unspoken embarrassment… After people saw me pick up a cup, kiss somebody… they felt like they knew me… A disabled person was in their living room."_ [_thestateofthearts.co.uk_](https://www.thestateofthearts.co.uk/features/profile-the-national-theatres-d-deaf-and-disabled-actors-database-one-year-on/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Playwright **Jack Thorne** echoes this. In his _Christmas Carol_, he insisted that **Tiny Tim** must be played by a young disabled actor. He quickly adds, though, that systemic resistance remains:

"You get push back on casting disabled actors a lot… it depends on the production as to whether I'm listened to." [BBC](https://www.bbc.com/culture/article/20191201-will-the-disabled-ever-get-the-stories-they-deserve?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Directors often frame their hesitation in terms of "authenticity" or "universality":

- Will the audience accept a disabled Hamlet?
- Will a disabled Cleopatra be seen as historically accurate?
- Will access needs compromise the rehearsal room?

These anxieties reveal less about the plays than about the industry's own narrow imagination. Disability is not a limitation on artistry but an expansion of it. Every disabled actor brings a distinctive physicality, rhythm, and worldview. To ignore this is to choose banality over innovation.

Meanwhile, UK theatre embraces gender-swapped casting, colour-conscious reimagining, and radical relocations of setting. Why, then, does disability remain the final frontier?

## The Creative Stagnation of Exclusion

Theatre thrives when it stretches boundaries — shocking us, reframing us. So why is disability treated as toxic metaphor rather than transformative presence?

On _Medium_, accessibility advocate **Zak Ford-Williams** nails it:

_"Authentic disability casting doesn't 'fix' absurdism, it deepens it… 'it stops being about disability — it starts being with disability.'"_ [_Medium_](https://medium.com/%40AccessibilityandInclusion/authentic-disability-casting-on-stage-why-it-matters-especially-in-absurdism-shakespeare-and-3c8af2a2fee9?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

And **Kaite O'Reilly**, a playwright and activist, captured the moral core of the debate:

_"Cripping-up is the twenty-first century's answer to blacking up… Theatres must 'put crips in our scripts.'"_ [_Wikipedia_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaite_O%27Reilly?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

## What We Don't See: Disability Without Definition

Beyond cripping-up, there's another kind of invisibility: **incidental casting** — disabled actors in roles not anchored by disability.

Lauren Bevan, spearheading [ProFile Performers](https://profileperformers.com/"%20\t%20"_blank) (a National Theatre database of D/deaf and disabled actors), notes:

"We've cast nine actors … in roles where their disability has been incidental and irrelevant to the role." [thestateofthearts.co.uk](https://www.thestateofthearts.co.uk/features/profile-the-national-theatres-d-deaf-and-disabled-actors-database-one-year-on/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Yet this innovation exists largely at fringe venues. On West End and National stages, even 24% of the UK population identifying as disabled finds no reflection in incidental casting.

**Why?** Because casting teams remain trapped in risk aversion, discomfort, and a narrow definition of "believability."

## The Vanishing Act of Incidental Casting

Equally troubling is what we rarely see: disabled actors in roles **without disability written into the script**. Incidental casting — where a disabled actor plays Juliet, Hamlet, or Hedda Gabler without textual comment — is vanishingly rare on major UK stages. Despite around 8% of the general public having a disability that is apparent and visible to the audience, in 2024 English Theatres cast around 1% of their roles incidentally with disabled actors, a clear indicator of how far disability inclusion in theatre has yet to go.

Instead of inclusion disabled performers are funnelled into "special projects" or festivals, rather than the classical and contemporary repertory that defines cultural prestige. The message is clear: disabled people are welcome when their presence is thematically convenient, but not when the role could "just as easily" be anyone.

This gap is not only a moral failing but an artistic one. Theatre thrives on surprise, disruption, and new perspectives. What richer opportunity exists than to reimagine a canonical role through the lived experience of a disabled performer?

## Voices from the Field

Reddit user (actor with hEDS): _"Many audition policies disallow accommodations… I fainted on stage because I was not allowed to sit, and \[they\] kicked me out…" → exposure of systemic ableism._ [_Reddit_](https://www.reddit.com/r/Theatre/comments/1iz8pfi?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Disabled actor (Backstage): _"As an actor with a disability playing a role that wasn't written specifically to have a disability, I can subtly reveal how the original work excluded people with disabilities… My disability isn't a costume I can take on and off…"_ [_Backstage_](https://www.backstage.com/magazine/article/stop-excluding-actors-disabilities-7387/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

These stories reaffirm what cripping-up and exclusion really cost: not just authenticity, but the possibility of change.

## A New Vision — Radical Inclusion, Genuine Casting

To shift culture, three steps are essential:

1. **End cripping-up by default**: cast disabled actors in disability-coded roles unless there's a compelling reason not to — especially in publicly funded theatres.
2. **Normalise incidental disabled casting**: see disabled performers in every walk-on, ingenue, or Shakespearean lover — not just the "disabled" ones.
3. **Value disability as artistry**: theatres must elevate the creative potential of disability, not treat it as an obstacle.

In the words of **Jenny Sealey**, artistic director of Graeae:

"We are not yet embraced fully within the DNA of the creative industry. Too often our inclusion feels like a 'vanity project.'" [The Stage](https://www.thestage.co.uk/features/features/should-only-disabled-actors-play-shakespeares-richard-iii-michelle-terry-arthur-hughes-adjoa-andoh-tom-mothersdale?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
