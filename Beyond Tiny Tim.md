# Beyond Tiny Tim. Disability as a Creative Force in — _A Christmas Carol: A Ghost Story_

Mark Gatiss's adaptation of A Christmas Carol, directed by Adam Penford, offers a compelling example of what can happen when disability is allowed to influence the interpretation of a familiar text rather than being contained within its most obviously disabled character.

The production does not abandon Tiny Tim's importance. Nor does it attempt to disguise the emotional force that Dickens placed around him. Instead, it changes the theatrical context in which Tim appears. Through the ensemble casting of disabled performer Zak Ford-Williams, disability is present across Scrooge's past and present, within the Cratchit household, among school children, on the streets of London and inside the social and economic structures the story examines.

Ford-Williams plays Tiny Tim, but he also plays a blind man named John, a pupil at Scrooge's school, Young Marley, an old business associate and Topper. These roles do not all portray disability in the same way. In some, disability is explicit. In others it is incidental. At times Ford-Williams uses his own movement and mobility within the character; elsewhere he masks aspects of his disability and performs a role that may be read as non-disabled. He also plays disabled characters whose impairments are not the same as his own, bringing a wider understanding of disabled experience without attempting to imitate a diagnosis.

The effect is cumulative. Disability ceases to be one character's narrative burden and becomes part of the production's creative vocabulary.

## A familiar story shaped by performance

A Christmas Carol has been adapted so frequently that audiences often arrive knowing not only the story but how they expect it to look.

Scrooge will be cold and isolated. The Cratchits will be warm but poor. The ghosts will reveal a series of moral truths. Tiny Tim will embody innocence and vulnerability. Scrooge's redemption will be measured partly through his concern for the child.

These expectations make the story theatrically useful, but they can also make it creatively restrictive. Once a character becomes culturally symbolic, the symbol can begin to replace the person. Tiny Tim is particularly susceptible to this. He is often remembered less as Timothy Cratchit, a child within a large and lively family, than as a shorthand for suffering, goodness and charitable feeling.

The problem is not simply that Tim is sentimentalised. It is that his disability is frequently used to demonstrate the moral character of everyone around him.

Bob's goodness is reinforced through his tenderness towards Tim. Mrs Cratchit's emotional life is expressed through her fear for him. Scrooge's former indifference becomes more disturbing when set against Tim's possible death. His eventual generosity becomes more meaningful because it may save the child. Tim can consequently become a test that other characters pass or fail. His body generates meaning, but his own capacity to create meaning can be lost.

Gatiss's writing, Penford's direction and Ford-Williams's performance do not completely remove this structure. Some of Tim's story is still mediated through Bob and Emily Cratchit. The family still worries about him, and Scrooge still learns through witnessing their circumstances. The production's achievement lies instead in surrounding this inherited structure with other images of disability, other kinds of character and other relationships between body and story.

By the time Ford-Williams appears as Tim, the audience has already encountered him elsewhere. His body cannot be held within one symbolic interpretation because the production has already shown that it can mean different things.

That is a significant creative intervention.

## The difference between casting a role and casting a production

There is an important distinction between casting a disabled actor as Tiny Tim and thinking about disability across the production as a whole.

The first decision concerns authenticity. It recognises that a disabled character should offer a meaningful employment opportunity to a disabled performer and that lived experience can bring detail, authority and specificity to the portrayal. This is a consideration show, or should be shown, to all roles identified as disabled, such as Richard III, Nessarose, Captain Ahab, Caliban, Tiresias and others.

The second decision is dramaturgical. It asks what disability can reveal about the world of the play.

A production can cast Tiny Tim authentically while leaving every other assumption untouched. Tim may remain the only disabled person in London. Disability may still signify childhood, dependency and impending death. The production may still treat non-disabled embodiment as the unspoken norm from which Tim deviates. But by casting Ford-Williams across the ensemble, A Ghost Story does something more searching. It makes disability mobile. It allows it to pass between different classes, ages, moral positions and dramatic functions.

This means that disability is not always doing the same kind of work. It does not always ask the audience for sympathy. It does not always need to be explained. It does not always define the character being played.

In one role, the performer's disability is central to the story. In another, it contributes to the character without becoming the scene's subject. In another, it is partly masked. In another, Ford-Williams draws on the wider experience of navigating a disabling society rather than matching a particular impairment.

The production therefore presents several approaches to disability portrayal inside a single performance.

## Tiny Tim: from emblem to particular child

Tiny Tim is Ford-Williams's most explicit and expected disabled role and the one most directly connected to the history of the story.

Casting Tim from within disabled experience changes both the employment opportunity and the audience's relationship with the character. For much of the character's theatrical and screen history, Tim's impairment provided an acting opportunity for non-disabled performers rather than employment for disabled actors. The character was recognised as disabled, but the performer's own disability was considered unnecessary, inconvenient or incompatible with professional production.

Authentic casting changes the relationship between the role and the audience. The performer does not temporarily borrow disabled movement as an external sign of vulnerability. His embodiment is not a visual effect that can be removed once the scene or performance is over.

But authenticity alone is not enough. A disabled performer can still be directed into a narrow or sentimental interpretation.

What matters in A Ghost Story is the detail of Tim's life within the family. He is capable of fatigue, but he is not defined by stillness. He requires support, but he is not excluded from activity. He is carried at times, yet he also stands, moves, eats, jokes, observes and participates. His relationships are not limited to being cared for. He is part of the family's collective rhythm.

One revealing moment occurs when Tim chooses not to follow the other children to listen to the Christmas pudding. He says that he is tired and remains behind. This could easily be played as a sorrowful indication of physical decline. Instead, it also creates space for Tim to make a decision about his own energy.

Rest becomes an action rather than an absence of action.

That distinction matters. Disabled people are often shown resting only when a narrative wants to demonstrate deterioration. In life, managing energy can be a form of knowledge, agency and self-preservation. Tim's choice need not be treated as surrender. It can show that he understands his body and acts accordingly.

His continued presence also changes the conversation between his parents. Rather than disappearing while adults discuss him, Tim remains part of the theatrical picture. He can hear their concern. He can register their glances. The audience must hold his own awareness alongside the parents' fear.

This does not give Tim complete control over how he is represented, but it makes it more difficult to treat him as an absent object of discussion.

The Cratchit meal develops this further. Tim is integrated into the scene's movement and celebration. His body contributes to the composition of the family rather than interrupting it. He is supported by the table and by those around him, but support does not diminish his theatrical authority.

The scene offers an image of interdependence. Everybody contributes to the meal, the conversation and the excitement. Tim's participation takes a particular form, but it is not a lesser one.

This is a more interesting dramatic proposition than either heroic independence or passive dependency. The family is held together by different forms of assistance, affection, labour and shared enjoyment. Tim makes those relationships visible, but he is not the only person who depends on them.

## John: disabled experience without diagnostic imitation

Ford-Williams also appears as John, a blind Londoner whose scenes include his companion Tom and Arthur, a guide dog represented through puppetry.

John is an invented character rather than a figure carried directly from Dickens's text. His scene contributes to the production's portrait of London and, more particularly, to its demonstration of Scrooge's behaviour before his transformation.

Scrooge tests whether John can see and then steals money from his collection tin. The action is mean, opportunistic and revealing. It shows Scrooge exploiting what he assumes to be another person's vulnerability. Bob Cratchit's later generosity towards John provides a moral contrast, but John's presence does more than establish which man is kinder. The scene expands the connection between disability and economic power.

John is not a member of Scrooge's family and does not possess Tim's symbolic innocence. He is an adult attempting to survive within the city. Scrooge does not merely fail to feel sorry for him. He uses a social and sensory difference as an opportunity for theft. The scene therefore brings ableism into direct contact with class and money. John is made vulnerable not by blindness alone but by the attitudes and actions of someone with greater social and economic power. Ford-Williams is not blind. This makes the role different from the authentic casting of Tiny Tim and raises an important question about what authenticity means.

A narrow interpretation would insist that every disabled character must be played by a performer with the same diagnosis or impairment. In some contexts, such matching may be essential, particularly where the role depends on specific cultural, sensory, linguistic or embodied knowledge. But diagnosis alone does not encompass everything that disabled people may share. Ford-Williams brings experience of being watched, underestimated, assisted, obstructed and interpreted by others. He understands the relationship between body, mobility, public space and social expectation. He also understands that an aid is not an abstract symbol but part of a person's interaction with the world.

This does not make his experience identical to blindness, and the production should not be read as claiming that it does. It means that the portrayal can be informed by disabled knowledge rather than constructed entirely from a non-disabled idea of what blindness looks like.

There is also a deliberately theatrical quality to the scene. Arthur (the dog) is a puppet, the production is visibly ensemble-based, and Ford-Williams is not asked to disappear inside a naturalistic impersonation. The audience is allowed to perceive both character and performance.

That openness matters. It reduces the pressure to produce a supposedly perfect visual copy of an impairment. John can be understood as a character created through theatrical convention, shaped by a performer who knows disability from within but is not claiming another person's exact experience.

## The school pupil: disability as ordinary participation

Ford-Williams appears as one of the pupils at Scrooge's school, using his wheelchair as the children race and leave for Christmas.

The moment may be brief, but its simplicity gives it force.

The pupil is not introduced as disabled. Nobody pauses to explain the chair. The scene is not about access, treatment, courage or prejudice. He is simply one of the children taking part.

This is incidental disability: visible, present and embodied, but not made responsible for the scene's narrative meaning.

At the same time, the casting does subtly affect that meaning. The children's speed, excitement and companionship are set against the isolation of the young Scrooge. A disabled child is located within the group that possesses freedom, friendship and joy, while the lonely child at the centre of the scene is presumed non-disabled. This reverses a familiar visual hierarchy. Disability is not used to represent deprivation. Social isolation is not attached to the wheelchair user. The tragedy belongs to Scrooge because he has been left alone.

The wheelchair also participates in the movement of the scene. It is not a static object that removes the performer from the production's physical language. It creates speed, direction and energy. This challenges a deeply embedded assumption in theatre: that movement belongs naturally to standing actors while wheelchair users require an adapted or reduced version of it.

Here, the chair is part of the action from the beginning. It does not need to imitate walking in order to signify freedom.

## Young Marley: masking, transformation and moral complexity

As Young Marley, Ford-Williams occupies a very different position.

In this role, elements of his disability are less visible. He stands, dances and participates in the physical life of Fezziwig's world. The character is not introduced as disabled and may be read by many audience members as non-disabled.

This is a rare form of masking within performance. It should not be confused with denying the performer's identity or pretending that disabled people should conceal themselves. Rather, it demonstrates something disabled actors are often not permitted to do: transform.

Non-disabled actors are routinely trusted to move across age, class, temperament and physical character. Their own bodies are treated as flexible instruments. Disabled actors, by contrast, are frequently assumed to be permanently attached to the visible meaning of their disability. Casting processes can behave as though a disabled performer must always play a disabled role and as though that role must always discuss disability.

Young Marley resists that restriction.

Ford-Williams is allowed to use technique, costume, movement and theatrical convention to create a character whose relationship to disability is not foregrounded. His disability does not prevent the audience from accepting him as a younger version of Marley, any more than another performer's height, facial structure or natural accent would prevent transformation.

At the same time, the audience cannot entirely forget the performer's other roles. Ensemble doubling allows traces of one character to accompany another.

This is especially important because Young Marley is connected to Scrooge's movement towards money, ambition and emotional withdrawal. Ford-Williams therefore embodies both Tim, whose future is threatened by the economic world Scrooge has created, and Marley, who helps draw Scrooge into that world.

Disability appears on both sides of the story's moral structure.

That is a more complex use of casting than making every disabled character virtuous. It refuses the idea that disability naturally signifies innocence, moral clarity or dependence. A disabled performer can embody complicity, ambition and authority as readily as tenderness or vulnerability.

The casting also disrupts any straightforward reading of disability as the opposite of capitalism. Young Marley is not excluded from economic power. He is one of its agents.

This does not make disability a metaphor for greed. The creative interest lies in preventing disability from being tied to any single moral meaning.

## Topper: disability, desire and social life

Topper participates in the games and flirtations at Fred's gathering. The character is socially involved, physically expressive and connected to desire. His presence does not require the production to stop and announce that disabled people can possess these qualities. They are communicated through casting and action.

The role can therefore be understood as incidental portrayal. Ford-Williams is a disabled performer playing a character whose dramatic purpose is not to represent disability.

Yet incidental does not mean insignificant.

Disabled performers have historically been channelled towards roles that emphasise innocence, dependency, bitterness or isolation. Casting a disabled actor as a flirtatious bachelor expands the range of associations available to both performer and audience.

It also changes the meaning of the games being played. Blind man's buff depends on temporarily simulating an impairment for entertainment. When Ford-Williams participates in the scene, the distinction between performed blindness, lived disability and theatrical convention becomes harder to ignore.

The production does not necessarily resolve the troubling implications of the game. Instead, his presence makes them less invisible. The audience may become more conscious of how easily disability is adopted as a temporary costume by people who can discard it.

At the same time, Ford-Williams is not positioned outside the social and sexual energy of the gathering. He is inside it.

This dual effect is valuable. Disability can expose an ableist convention while the disabled performer remains entitled to pleasure, mischief and desire.

## One performer, several kinds of portrayal

Taken together, Ford-Williams's roles demonstrate that disability portrayal is not a single technique.

As Tiny Tim, he plays an explicitly disabled character whose experience is connected to the performer's own lived embodiment.

As the school pupil, disability is present without requiring narrative explanation.

As John, Ford-Williams plays a disabled character whose impairment differs from his own, drawing on wider lived knowledge rather than attempting a clinical reconstruction.

As Young Marley, aspects of disability are masked or recoded so that the performer can inhabit a character not understood through disability.

As Topper, the actor's disability coexists with a role concerned with social participation and desire rather than impairment.

These approaches are not interchangeable. Each carries different responsibilities. Each asks something different of the performer and audience. Their strength comes from being used together.

If Ford-Williams had appeared only as Tim, audiences could have absorbed his disability into the existing emotional machinery of the story. Because he appears repeatedly, no single interpretation is sufficient.

The audience must keep adjusting.

At one moment, they may read his body through vulnerability. At another, through speed. At another, through economic ambition. At another, through sexuality. At another, they may barely consider disability at all.

This instability is creatively productive. It interrupts the habit of treating disabled people as immediately knowable from their bodies.

## The actor's body as a link between characters

Ensemble casting always creates connections that exceed the written text.

An audience may consciously recognise that one performer is playing several roles, or it may experience the relationship more instinctively. A gesture, voice, physical rhythm or silhouette carries a trace of the actor's earlier appearance.

Ford-Williams's casting creates a network across Scrooge's life.

The disabled school pupil belongs to Scrooge's childhood. Young Marley is connected to his developing economic identity. John encounters his cruelty in the present. Topper belongs to the social warmth Scrooge has rejected. Tim represents both the consequences of his choices and the possibility of another way of living.

The same performer moves through these different times and social spaces.

Disability therefore becomes part of the production's structure of haunting. It is present across memory, current behaviour and possible futures. It cannot be contained within the Cratchit household or treated as a single problem Scrooge will later solve.

This creates a productive tension around responsibility. Scrooge's conversion is not simply a promise to help one disabled child. It becomes an awakening to a society already populated by people he has ignored, exploited or failed to imagine.

The production does not need to state this directly. Casting does the work.

## Disability and the social meaning of money

A Christmas Carol is often described as a story about generosity, but its politics are more complicated than the conversion of a miser into a charitable man.

Money determines who has warmth, food, healthcare, leisure and security. It affects who can rest, who must work and whose future can be protected. Disability makes these questions more visible because support and access are rarely distributed independently of wealth.

Tiny Tim's possible death is often framed as the tragic consequence of his physical condition. Yet the Cratchits' poverty is equally important. Their limited resources shape the support, treatment and material comfort available to him.

A wealthier disabled child might inhabit the same body within very different circumstances.

This is where the production's wider casting becomes especially useful. By placing disability in several economic and social positions, it prevents a simple equation between disability and poverty.

John is economically vulnerable. The school pupil is part of a joyful group. Young Marley moves towards wealth and influence. Topper belongs to Fred's comfortable social circle. Tim lives within a loving but financially constrained family.

Disability crosses class, even though class changes how it is experienced.

This opens another possibility that lies beyond the particular production: Scrooge himself could be played by a disabled actor.

Such casting would need to avoid suggesting that disability caused his cruelty or emotional withdrawal. Its creative value would lie elsewhere. A wealthy disabled Scrooge might possess forms of access, equipment and assistance unavailable to Tim or John. He might understand his own needs while remaining indifferent to the barriers faced by others.

That contrast could sharpen the story's examination of privilege. Shared disability does not automatically create solidarity. Economic security can protect one disabled person while leaving another exposed.

The more interesting question is therefore not whether Scrooge feels sorry for Tim. It is whether he comes to recognise the systems from which he benefits and the lives affected by his decisions.

## Care without reducing a character to care

The Cratchit family is central to the production's handling of support.

Bob carries Tim. Family members adjust around him. His parents worry about his energy and future. These actions could easily become sentimental images in which Tim exists primarily as the recipient of care.

Penford's staging, Gatiss's adaptation and Ford-Williams's performance complicate this by preserving Tim's involvement in the family's activity.

Care does not place him outside the group. It is one of the ways the group functions.

This distinction is important because theatrical representations often treat independence as the only route to agency. A disabled character is considered strong when they require nothing from anyone, while accepting assistance is portrayed as defeat.

The Cratchits offer another model. Support and participation coexist. Tim can be carried and still influence the scene. He can rest and remain dramatically present. He can use a crutch, furniture or another person without becoming an object.

The family's joy also prevents care from being represented only as sacrifice. They are anxious about Tim, but their life with him is not presented as a tragedy. The Christmas meal moves quickly between concern, noise, appetite and celebration.

Their priorities are relational. Tim matters because he is Tim, not because his suffering gives the family moral status.

## Movement, stillness and theatrical authority

The production repeatedly questions what counts as meaningful stage movement.

Ford-Williams appears standing, seated, supported, dancing and using a wheelchair. None of these positions defines the limit of his performance.

This variability is important because theatre has inherited a narrow idea of physical neutrality. The supposedly neutral performer stands evenly, moves without visible effort and can be placed anywhere in the director's composition. Bodies that require support, use aids or move asymmetrically are often treated as deviations from this imagined norm.

But neutrality is itself a creative choice. It privileges one kind of body and then conceals the privilege by calling it ordinary.

Ford-Williams's presence makes that convention visible. His movement cannot always be separated from the objects, surfaces and people around him. The table at the Cratchit meal, for example, can provide stability while also becoming part of the choreography. A wheelchair can create velocity. A crutch can contribute to gesture and rhythm. Being carried can communicate intimacy and trust.

These possibilities are not compensations for an absent non-disabled performance. They are theatrical resources.

The production's physical language becomes more varied because the performer's body generates different relationships with space.

This also affects stillness. When Tim rests, the audience is not necessarily watching a performer who has ceased to act. Attention, breath, listening and the management of energy remain active. Stillness can hold knowledge and tension.

A production willing to understand this can discover forms of stage presence that conventional movement direction may overlook.

## Challenging the habits of ableist interpretation

Ableism in theatre is not confined to openly hostile attitudes. It is also present in assumptions that are rarely questioned.

One is the belief that a disabled actor can play only a character whose disability is written into the text.

Another is that a disabled body will always dominate the audience's attention and therefore must be narratively explained.

A third is that historical settings are naturally non-disabled unless evidence proves otherwise.

There is also the expectation that disabled characters should be morally sympathetic, physically limited, romantically marginal or economically dependent.

A Ghost Story unsettles each of these assumptions.

Ford-Williams plays roles that are and are not defined by disability. He appears in a historical world without requiring a special justification for his existence. He participates in energetic movement. He portrays childhood, adulthood, companionship, economic ambition and flirtation. He plays characters who receive care and characters who influence others.

His presence also challenges the idea that impairment can be accurately understood by looking.

The audience cannot assume that the performer's use of a wheelchair describes everything he can do. They see him stand and dance. Nor can they assume that standing makes him non-disabled. His body moves between different modes without becoming more or less authentic.

This is particularly significant in relation to ambulatory wheelchair use. Public understanding often depends on a false division between people who walk and people who use wheelchairs. In reality, many people use chairs because of fatigue, pain, balance, distance or fluctuating capacity while retaining some ability to stand or walk.

The production does not need to stop and provide this explanation. Ford-Williams's performance embodies the complexity.

It asks the audience to abandon the demand that disability remain visually consistent for their convenience.

## Refusing a neat cure

The ending of A Christmas Carol has often encouraged adaptations to imply that Tim's future happiness depends on becoming non-disabled.

Dickens confirms that Tim survives, but he does not clearly describe the removal of Tim's impairment. The cultural memory of the story has nevertheless often folded survival, cure and Scrooge's generosity into one reassuring conclusion.

Gatiss's adaptation introduces an older Tim through the narrator. This might initially suggest that the child has outgrown or been cured of disability, particularly because another performer embodies the older man in his late 70s who has altered his movement and speaking voice to that of an older Zak Ford-Williams.

The production's conclusion complicates that reading. Ford-Williams remains visibly disabled within the final company and uses his wheelchair. His disability is not dropped when the story reaches its celebratory resolution, nor is it abandoned when the performers acknowledge the audience.

This prevents the ending from offering non-disability as the ultimate proof that everything has been put right.

Tim can live. He can grow older. He can become a storyteller. Scrooge's generosity can improve his material circumstances. None of this requires the production to erase disabled embodiment.

The final image therefore matters as much as the narrative information. Theatre communicates through what remains visible after the plot has been resolved.

## Employment as part of the creative achievement

There is another dimension to the production that should not be separated from its artistic interpretation.

Casting creates employment.

Disabled characters have existed throughout theatre history, but they have not consistently generated work for disabled performers. The roles have often been used to display the versatility of non-disabled actors, while disabled actors have been judged too specific for characters not explicitly written as disabled.

Ford-Williams's ensemble casting challenges both practices at once.

He is employed to play an established disabled character, but he is not confined to that character. His casting recognises his authority in relation to disability while also recognising his range as an actor.

This is an important distinction. Inclusion should not mean admitting disabled performers into a small category of approved roles. It should expand the range of creative and professional opportunities available to them.

The production gains from that expansion. Ford-Williams's roles create connections, contradictions and images that would not exist if Tiny Tim were treated as a separate casting exercise.

Employment and artistic quality are not competing concerns here. The employment decision produces the creative result.

## Guidance for Directors and Casting Directors

The value of A Christmas Carol: A Ghost Story lies not in providing a formula that other productions should reproduce. Its deeper lesson is that disability can be used to ask better questions of a text.

The following guidance is drawn from the production's creative approach and can be applied both to A Christmas Caroland to other classical, historical and contemporary works.

### 1\. Begin with the whole world of the play

Do not restrict the disability conversation to characters whose impairments are explicitly identified.

Examine the society represented by the text. Consider its families, workplaces, crowds, institutions, schools, households and social gatherings. Ask where disabled people are likely to be present and why a production might have learned not to imagine them there.

The objective is not to add disability indiscriminately. It is to stop treating non-disabled embodiment as the automatic starting point.

### 2\. Distinguish between different forms of portrayal

Not every disabled performer needs to be cast in the same way.

A production may include:

· an explicitly disabled character played by a disabled performer;

· a role in which the actor's disability influences character and staging without becoming the main subject;

· incidental casting where disability is visible but narratively unexplained;

· a character whose disability differs from the performer's, approached through shared lived knowledge rather than diagnosis imitation;

· a role in which the performer masks or transforms aspects of their disability;

· a character normally assumed to be non-disabled but reconsidered through disabled casting.

These approaches carry different creative opportunities and responsibilities. They should be chosen consciously rather than collapsed into a single category of representation.

### 3\. Do not make one actor carry the entire subject

When a single disabled character appears in an otherwise non-disabled world, that character may acquire excessive symbolic weight.

Consider whether disability could be distributed across several roles, generations, classes or moral positions. This can reduce the expectation that one performer must represent every disabled person while allowing a wider and more credible range of characterisation.

It can also produce better drama. Disabled characters should be able to disagree with one another, possess different privileges and make contrasting moral choices.

### 4\. Cast across the moral range of the story

Disabled actors should not be considered only for characters who are innocent, vulnerable or inspirational.

Explore roles associated with authority, ambition, sexuality, comedy, selfishness, intellect, danger, wealth and contradiction.

A disabled performer playing a morally complicated character does not create a negative representation simply because the character behaves badly. Difficulty arises when disability itself is used as the visual explanation for corruption or cruelty.

The character should be complex because people are complex, not because disability is being used as shorthand for moral failure.

### 5\. Use doubling as a form of interpretation

In ensemble work, examine the meanings created when one performer plays several characters.

Ask what travels between the roles. Consider whether the same actor might appear in Scrooge's childhood, his economic life, his current social world and the future he fears.

A disabled performer's roles do not all need to portray disability in the same way. The contrast between them may be the source of the most interesting interpretation.

### 6\. Separate authenticity from diagnosis

Authenticity is not achieved solely by matching a performer and character according to a medical label.

Where a role depends on a specific culture, language, sensory experience or form of embodiment, close matching may be essential. In other cases, a disabled performer may bring relevant knowledge of access, assistance, public scrutiny, bodily variability and social barriers without sharing the character's precise impairment.

The production should be honest about the distinction. It should not claim that all disabled experiences are interchangeable, but neither should it reduce lived experience to a diagnostic checklist.

### 7\. Allow disabled actors to transform

Do not assume that every role played by a disabled performer must be rewritten as explicitly disabled.

Acting involves transformation. Disabled actors should be permitted to mask, alter or redirect aspects of their own embodiment where this is safe and creatively appropriate, just as other actors transform voice, age, posture and movement.

At the same time, masking should not be required as the price of employment. It should be one creative option among several, developed collaboratively with the performer.

### 8\. Design movement from the bodies in the room

Do not create choreography for an imagined non-disabled company and then adapt it after casting.

Begin with the performers who are present. Explore how wheelchairs, crutches, furniture, support, stillness, different energy levels and varied forms of movement can shape the production's rhythm and visual language.

A mobility aid is not merely a practical necessity. It may contribute speed, sound, line, character, status and relationship.

The performer should lead decisions about how their body and equipment are used.

### 9\. Give disabled characters agency within scenes about them

When another character discusses a disabled person's health, future or support, consider where the disabled character is and what they understand.

They may listen, react, resist, redirect attention or choose not to respond. Silence can be active. Rest can be chosen. Assistance can be negotiated.

Do not assume that agency requires physical independence or lengthy dialogue.

### 10\. Examine the relationship between disability and resources

Ask how wealth, housing, employment, family support and access to services affect the character's circumstances.

Avoid presenting the body as the sole source of difficulty when poverty, prejudice or inaccessible environments are equally important.

In A Christmas Carol, this means considering not only Tim's health but what the Cratchits can afford, what support is available and how Scrooge's economic decisions shape their lives.

### 11\. Preserve joy, appetite and desire

Disabled characters should be allowed to enjoy food, games, celebration, friendship, romance and physical expression.

These elements should not be included merely to prove that disabled people can be happy. They should arise because the characters are participants in the life of the play.

Joy is particularly valuable when it exists alongside fatigue, uncertainty or support rather than being used to deny them.

### 12\. Question the ending

Review what the production presents as resolution.

Does survival become confused with cure? Does a mobility aid disappear once the story becomes hopeful? Does the final tableau imply that a character's life has improved because disability is no longer visible?

A positive ending can be created through security, belonging, access, care and opportunity. It does not require the removal of disability.

The curtain call should also be considered part of this meaning. It should not reveal disabled embodiment to have been only a temporary theatrical disguise.

### 13\. Make access part of the creative process

Accessible auditions, rehearsals and performances should not be treated as administrative support added around the art.

They determine who can participate and what the production can discover.

Provide material in accessible formats, describe movement requirements by their purpose rather than by one fixed method, invite access conversations early and ensure that venues, schedules and communication practices support the performers being considered.

Access can create new methods, rhythms and staging possibilities. It should enable experimentation rather than merely prevent exclusion.

### 14\. Treat casting as both interpretation and opportunity

Every casting decision shapes the audience's understanding of the text and determines who is able to work.

These responsibilities should not be separated. A disabled performer should not be employed solely because the production wishes to communicate inclusion, but neither should employment inequality be dismissed as unrelated to creative practice.

The most successful decisions will do both: create substantial work for disabled performers and reveal possibilities within the text that the production would otherwise have missed.

## Conclusion

Mark Gatiss's adaptation, Adam Penford's direction and Zak Ford-Williams's performance demonstrate that the creative potential of disability in A Christmas Carol extends far beyond the authentic casting of Tiny Tim.

Ford-Williams's presence reshaped the production because it is permitted to be variable. He plays disability directly, incidentally and metaphorically. He draws on lived experience without being confined to one diagnosis. He masks aspects of his disability in one role and makes them central in another. He embodies vulnerability, companionship, economic ambition, social participation and desire.

The result challenges the assumption that a disabled actor's body has one stable meaning.

It also reveals something important about adaptation. Familiar texts do not remain alive simply because they are repeatedly staged. They remain alive when creative decisions expose something in them that audiences have learned not to see.

Disability can be one of those decisions.

Not because every character must become disabled, and not because disability automatically makes a production innovative, but because casting a wider range of bodies can disturb inherited assumptions about who belongs in a story, what a character can signify and how theatrical meaning is made.

In A Ghost Story, disability does not change the destination of Dickens's narrative. Scrooge is still haunted, transformed and returned to society. The Cratchits still celebrate. Tim still lives.

What changes is the world through which that story travels.

It becomes a world in which disability is not confined to a single child waiting to be saved. It is already present in childhood, work, friendship, poverty, wealth, memory, desire and community.

Once that possibility has been seen in A Christmas Carol, it becomes difficult not to look for it in every other text.
