# Authentic Disability Casting on Stage: Why It Matters — Especially in Absurdism, Shakespeare, and Historical Drama

_When we cast disability authentically, we don't narrow theatre, we expand it. Disability stops being metaphor and becomes truth. The stage becomes more than inclusive, it becomes real._

## Disability in Theatre: A Long Shadow

Theatre has long featured disabled characters, whether in classics like [Richard III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_III_%28play%29), [Endgame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endgame_%28play%29), [The Tempest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Tempest), [Coram Boy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coram_Boy_%28play%29), [Oedipus Rex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oedipus_Rex) or [The Glass Menagerie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Glass_Menagerie), or in plays about historic figures such as Lord Byron, Frida Kahlo, Franz Kafka or Joseph Merrick. Yet more often than not, non-disabled actors play these roles**,** an ableist practice known as [cripping-up](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cripping-up), that overlooks lived experience and purposefully restricts opportunity for disabled performers.

The issue goes beyond exclusion; it also shapes the stories being told. When disability is only ever imagined, it risks becoming symbolic, reductive, or even exploitative. And nowhere is this tension more visible than in works of **absurdism**, **historical theatre**, and **Shakespearean drama**.

## Absurdism and the Postmodern Potential of Disability

Absurdist theatre, as pioneered by Beckett, Genet, and Ionesco, often leans on grotesque physicality and fragmentation of the self. Disability is frequently used as metaphor: Hamm is blind and cannot walk; Nagg and Nell are legless in ash bins (Endgame); Lucky's incoherent monologue (Waiting for Godot) hints at cognitive decline or trauma. Even Winnie, buried waist-deep in Beckett's Happy Days, lives in a state of immobilised cheerfulness, simultaneously comic, tragic, and dehumanised.

This symbolic approach, though once groundbreaking, now demands critique. When disability is used merely as metaphor, it can reinforce pity tropes or objectifying representation. But when cast authentically, it can unlock entirely new dimensions.

Actor and advocate [Zak Ford-Williams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zak_Ford-Williams) argues that disabled casting within absurdism renders the genre postmodern. "It stops being about disability," he says. "It starts being with disability." In this shift, the metaphor collapses into presence. The audience can no longer pretend that disability is just symbolic, it's real, embodied, lived.

A vision disabled Hamm in Endgame or a physically Winnie or Willie in Happy Days turns abstraction into realism, dissonance into meaning. The performance stops pointing at disability and instead points from it.

Authentic disability casting doesn't "fix" absurdism, it deepens it.

## Historical Plays and the Normalcy of Disability

In historical dramas, especially those set before modern medicine, disability was not rare, nor was it necessarily a source of social isolation. War injuries, plagues, poor sanitation, and harsh labour made disability a visible part of daily life. It was often a trait**,** not an identity.  
Yet modern productions often erase this. Costumes are period-accurate, accents are dialect-accurate, but bodies? They remain suspiciously sanitized.  
By reintroducing disability into these works, whether through characters or casting directors restore historical truth and challenge contemporary norms. A courtier with a limp, a soldier with missing fingers, a merchant who stammers, these aren't anachronisms, they're reminders of **real people** history left behind.  
Authentic casting helps restore disability as part of human continuity, not a modern invention or symbolic inconvenience.

## Disability in Shakespeare: Beyond the Text

Shakespeare's canon is filled with characters whose impairments — physical, sensory, or cognitive — are central to how they are perceived but not necessarily who they are:

- **Richard III**, with scoliosis or a spinal deformity, with the visibility of the condition heightened in the text.
- **Caliban**, depicted as "deformed" and "monstrous."
- **Lavinia**, who loses her tongue and hands in Titus Andronicus.
- **Thersites**, **Lancelot Gobbo**, **Falstaff,** all described in terms of bodily difference or perceived foolishness. "Gobbo" is even an Italian slang word from the period for hunchback.

These characters are complex, multifaceted and so should their portrayals be. Too often, disability in Shakespeare is flattened into villainy or pathos. Authentic casting pushes back against this, revealing **agency, depth,** and **contradiction**.  
The issue is that we look at these texts through a modern lens of disability, whereas because of disease, work injury, war and other factors, in Shakespeare's time disability was more commonplace and every day. A characteristic of a person and not an identity.

Consider [Esmond Knight](http://www.esmondknight.org.uk/page/), a celebrated British actor who continued performing major roles at the Riyal Shakespeare Company (1948), and others, like Lear and Claudius after becoming legally blind during WWII. His condition didn't diminish his art, it transformed it and he appeared in all three of Oliviers Shakespearean films, evert time playing non-disabled roles.

Casting a blind Lear or a Hamlet with mild cerebral palsy doesn't limit interpretation, it expands it. It challenges audiences to confront their own assumptions about perception, control, and fragility that are already embedded in the text. Disability belongs in Shakespeare, not just as metaphor, but as presence.

## Why Authentic and Incidental Disability Casting Matters

_"Creativity is about making interesting choices, and casting a disabled actor can be an interesting choice."  
Zak Ford-Williams_

**1\. It deepens performances**  
Lived experience brings detail, contradiction, and truth to every role whether written as disabled or not. The disability should be apparent to the audience either through visible disability characteristics or signifiers, or through notes in the programme,

**2\. It creates equity**  
Disabled actors remain vastly underrepresented on stage and screen and [cripping-up](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cripping-up), a form of discrimination in casting, is still commonplace. Authentic casting corrects long-standing exclusion.

**3\. It avoids cliché**  
Without authentic voices, disability becomes symbolic, tragic, or inspirational. Authentic and incidental casting breaks this cycle and is creatively interesting to audiences.

**4\. It opens the canon**  
Disabled actors can and should play any role. Casting against assumption brings new ideas and emotional clarity to old texts.

_"Some have found it interesting how the production_ (Antony and Cleopatra) _uses a style of signing to match the richness of Shakespeare's play. An example is how the sign for 'death' used by Cleopatra in the play symbolises how her character is connected to both Egypt and Rome, with the sign reflecting both the Egyptian sense of death — with the body being committed to the earth — and the Roman sense of the spirit soaring to the sky."_[_Limping Chicken blog._](https://limpingchicken.com/2024/09/05/i-cant-believe-im-on-that-stage-again-nadia-nadarajah-on-starring-as-cleopatra-at-shakespeares-globe-bsl/)

Authentic disability casting is not a trend, it's a creative imperative.

**When absurdist theatre becomes postmodern through embodiment…  
When history becomes whole again through disabled presence…  
When Shakespeare speaks through real disabled lives…**

Then the theatre is not only more inclusive it is more truthful, more daring, and more alive. Let disability be more than metaphor. Let it be presence, power, and performance.
