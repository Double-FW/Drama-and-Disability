# Reclaiming Richard III: Age, Disability, and the Future of Shakespeare

## Richard III: The Role, the Legacy, the Body

The figure of Richard III has stalked the Shakespearean stage for over four centuries, a villain, a king, and crucially, a man written as disabled. As debates rage over who should play whom in classical theatre, Richard stands at the burning crossroads of representation, tradition, and change.

For years, the question of who plays Richard III has largely been about age or gender. Now, increasingly, it's about disability. And the parallels to the racial casting evolution of Othello are striking and instructive.

## A Role Defined by Misrepresentation

Written with one of the largest speaking parts in all of Shakespeare, Richard III presents its protagonist as both physically and morally deformed — "rudely stamped," "unfinished," and "cheated of feature."

Historically, the real King Richard had scoliosis. But Shakespeare's Richard is a concoction of Tudor propaganda and ableist metaphor. And for centuries, the role has rewarded able-bodied actors for donning disability like costume — a practice known as "[cripping-up](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cripping-up)."

## The Numbers Tell a Story

A newly compiled dataset of over 100 UK productions since 1900 shows two disturbing trends:

**Age inaccuracy** is common. Richard III died at 32. Yet many actors — Ian McKellen (51), Ralph Fiennes (54), Mark Rylance (53) — played him much older.

**Gender fluidity** is an interesting creative choice, as bringing in different gender perspectives to roles like Richard or Hamlet have opened-up creative possibility.

**Disability exclusion** is the norm. in the last 100 professional productions, only 8 cast disabled actors, and only in very recent years. Yet Richard's disability is central to the play.

**Creatives often demand authenticity when it flatters the able-bodied but dismiss it when it empowers the disabled,** which has been evident in interviews with more than one eminent stage actor.

## From Cripping-Up to Cripping-In

"Cripping-up" — where non-disabled actors perform disability through prosthetics or mimicry — is increasingly condemned. It reduces lived experience to performance and makes disability a prop for plot.

But the tide is turning:

- **Arthur Hughes** (2022, RSC): The first disabled actor to play Richard for the Royal Shakespeare Company. His performance was hailed as "charming, dangerous, and lucid."
- **Michael Patrick** (2024, Lyric Belfast): Living with MND, he reframed Richard's ambition as a response to terminal illness.
- **Zak Ford-Williams** (2024): At 25, he brought "quiet rage" and emotional complexity, rejecting stereotype in favour of subtle intensity.

"Zak Ford-Williams brought a quiet rage I've never seen before. Thank you for this." — Audience comment

This isn't tokenism. It's transformation.

## Lessons from Othello

Up until the 1970s, Othello was still played by blacking-up (AKA blackface). Today, a white Othello is considered unthinkable. Why? Because representation matters. Authenticity matters. Power matters.

Black actors redefined Othello — not just by taking the role, but by reshaping it.

Richard III is next.

## Creative Freedom Through Authentic Casting

Casting disabled actors doesn't limit imagination — it expands it:

- [**Ramps on the Moon**](https://rampsonthemoon.co.uk), [**Graeae**](https://graeae.org)**,** [**Box of Tricks**](https://boxoftrickstheatre.co.uk) and other [disability led theatres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_and_disability#Disabled_talent_and_theatre_companies) show how inclusivity enhances storytelling, and have given disabled people an opportunity to access training, whereas up until no more than a decade ago disabled students were actively excluded from Drama Schools.
- [**ProFile Performers**](https://profileperformers.com) is a platform developed by the Royal National Theatre to showcase the work and abilities of disabled actors.

Meanwhile, the [**Globe's 2024 production**](https://www.theguardian.com/culture/2024/jan/31/richard-iii-non-disabled-actor-michelle-terry-shakespeare-globe-theatre) controversially cast a non-disabled actor without following any inclusive creative process, sparking protests. The message was clear: **Disability is not incidental to Richard anymore. It is intrinsic.**

## What to Theatres Need to Change?

1. **Stop cripping-up when casting** [**all disabled roles**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_and_disability#Examples_of_scripted_disabled_characters_in_plays)**, not just Richard.**
2. **Cast disability incidentally, and to help identify which ones** [**bring in a disabled actor or director as a dramaturg**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_and_disability#Disability_dramaturgy)**.**
3. **Produce inclusive casting policies and embed inclusive practices in all productions, such as** [**the policy created by the Royal Shakespeare Company**](https://www.rsc.org.uk/about-us/policies/equity-diversity-justice-and-inclusion)**.**
4. **Ensure rehearsal and performance spaces are accessible, and that there is an accessibility consultant involved in any planning.**
5. **Most importantly, embrace disability as artistic possibility, not limitation or a diversity box to be ticked.**

"Putting a disabled actor on a stage or screen is a postmodernist act in itself" — Zak Ford-Williams.

## Shakespeare Isn't a Museum

Authentic casting is not about policing creativity. It's about liberating it.

It's time to stop treating Richard's body as a metaphor — and start treating its portrayal as an intrinsic part of his identity.
