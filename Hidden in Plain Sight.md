# Hidden in Plain Sight: A History of Disabled Performers in Hollywood

Cinema did not discover disabled actors recently. They were there before movies learned to speak.

Long before inclusion became an industry objective, before diversity standards, access coordinators or arguments about authentic casting, Deaf and disabled performers were already working in the new medium of film. Some became stars. Others built substantial careers as character actors, stunt performers and supporting players. A few changed the language of cinema itself.

Their history presents Hollywood with an uncomfortable contradiction. For more than a century, filmmakers have been fascinated by disability. Disabled characters have been heroes, villains, geniuses, monsters, victims, lovers, soldiers, detectives and comic relief. Disability has generated Oscar-winning performances and some of cinema's most memorable stories. Yet disabled actors themselves have repeatedly struggled to gain access to those roles, and have been considered even less often for characters whose scripts say nothing about disability at all.

The problem has never been a lack of disabled characters. It has been a lack of imagination about who is allowed to play them.

## Before Hollywood learned to speak

The silent era offers one of the most striking contradictions in this history because early cinema was, in some respects, unusually open to Deaf performers. Silent film depended upon gesture, facial expression, physical rhythm and visual communication. Dialogue appeared as intertitles rather than sound. For Deaf actors and audiences, this new art form did not automatically impose the barriers that arrived later with synchronised speech.

Among the Deaf performers working during this period were [Granville Redmond](https://www.imdb.com/name/nm0715056/), [Emerson Romero](https://www.imdb.com/name/nm16144736), Louis Weinberg, who performed as David Marvel, and Albert Ballin. Their careers are now difficult to reconstruct because so much silent cinema has been lost, but their existence challenges the idea that Deaf participation in screen drama is a modern development. Historical research into Deaf cinema records a small but genuine community of Deaf performers working within the emerging film industry.

Granville Redmond is particularly fascinating. Better remembered today as an important California landscape painter, Redmond was Deaf and also appeared in several films associated with his friend Charlie Chaplin. Chaplin admired the expressive possibilities of signed communication, and Redmond moved between painting, performance and the creative world surrounding Chaplin's studio. Their friendship is a reminder that physical expression in silent cinema was not simply exaggerated acting: it was a sophisticated visual language, and Deaf experience existed close to its development.

Emerson Romero followed another extraordinary path. Performing under the screen name Tommy Albert, he appeared in more than two dozen short comedies during the late silent era. When sound transformed the industry, opportunities for Deaf actors contracted and films themselves became less accessible to Deaf audiences. Romero later responded to that exclusion by experimenting with inserting written captions into sound films, pioneering an approach that helped anticipate later captioning systems. An actor pushed out by technological change became part of the history of making that technology accessible again.

There is a larger lesson here. Technological progress is not automatically inclusive progress.

The arrival of sound made cinema richer in one sense while simultaneously narrowing who could participate in it. Hollywood did not lose Deaf talent because Deaf people suddenly became less capable of acting. The rules of the medium changed around them, and the industry largely failed to redesign opportunity with them in mind.

That pattern would repeat itself throughout film history.

## Freaks: visibility that Hollywood found difficult to look at

Tod Browning's Freaks in 1932 remains one of the most complicated landmarks in the history of disabled performance.

The film cannot simply be celebrated as an enlightened piece of representation. Its title belongs to the language of the sideshow, and its performers worked within a culture that often-turned bodily difference into spectacle. Yet Browning did something Hollywood has remarkably rarely repeated at the same scale: he placed a large community of genuinely disabled and physically different performers at the centre of a major studio production.

Johnny Eck, the Hilton sisters, Harry and Daisy Earles, Schlitzie and other sideshow performers appear not merely as anonymous background bodies but as members of a community with friendships, relationships, humour, loyalties, rivalries, love and collective power. More radically, the film's moral structure does not make the visibly different characters its true monsters. The cruelty comes largely from conventionally 'attractive', non-disabled characters who exploit them.

Audiences and censors reacted strongly. MGM substantially cut the film, and Britain banned it for decades. The BFI notes that Browning's decision to cast actual performers with visible disabilities provoked revulsion among contemporary audiences despite the film's broadly sympathetic treatment of them.

What followed is telling.

Hollywood could have concluded that audiences needed to become more accustomed to seeing disabled people as complex human beings. Instead, it largely retreated. Freaks therefore represent both possibility and warning. It proved that disabled performers could populate an entire dramatic world, but it also exposed how uncomfortable mainstream culture was when disability could not be disguised, simulated or kept safely at the edges of the frame.

### The Wizard of Oz: extraordinary visibility inside a very narrow door

Seven years later, The Wizard of Oz created another enormous visibility moment.

Around 124 little people, people of short stature and other performers were assembled to populate Munchkinland, supplemented by some children of average height. For many actors of short stature, the production created employment, community and an opportunity to participate in one of the largest films Hollywood had attempted. Some performers described meeting other little people in significant numbers for the first time. Decades later, the Munchkin performers were collectively honoured with a star on the Hollywood Walk of Fame.

That achievement deserves recognition. But it also reveals the narrowness of Hollywood's imagination. A studio could find work for more than a hundred performers of short stature when it needed to construct a fantastical population. The same industry was far less interested in casting those performers as journalists, teachers, neighbours, lawyers, criminals, parents, shopkeepers or romantic partners.

They were visible because their difference was required.

This distinction runs through the history of disability casting. Visibility is not the same as equality. An industry can place disabled people prominently on screen while still limiting them to a remarkably small range of imaginative possibilities. For actors of short stature in particular, fantasy became both an opportunity and a cage. Fairy-tale kingdoms, alien worlds, circuses and magical creatures generated employment, but the same performers remained largely absent from the everyday social world Hollywood presented as normal.

The problem was never that audiences could not see them. It was that film directors struggled to imagine them anywhere else. Directors are a group of people renowned for having open and creative imaginations, however by enlarge that seems to disappear when it comes to the creative casting of disabled actors.

## The actors who disproved the rules

Alongside these moments of collective visibility were individual performers whose careers quietly dismantled assumptions about what disabled actors could do.

Harold Lloyd lost part of his right hand in a 1919 studio accident yet continued to become one of silent cinema's defining physical comedians, concealing the impairment on screen with a prosthetic glove. His career demonstrates one version of Hollywood inclusion: disability could be accommodated when it could also be made invisible.

Lionel Barrymore presents another, more radical example. By the late 1930s, arthritis and injuries had substantially affected his mobility and he increasingly used a wheelchair. Rather than ending his career, his physical reality was incorporated into his work. He continued playing substantial characters, including Dr Gillespie in the Dr Kildare films and, most famously, the formidable Mr Potter in It's a Wonderful Life. Turner Classic Movies records that he continued acting from his wheelchair after becoming partially paralysed.

Barrymore is important because his wheelchair did not remove his authority as a performer. He could still be intelligent, threatening, powerful, funny or emotionally complicated. His characters were not automatically reduced to stories about overcoming disability.

There is, however, another lesson hidden inside his success. Hollywood proved perfectly capable of adapting productions when the actor was Lionel Barrymore. The industry often finds flexibility when it already values the performer. What it has historically been less willing to do is offer that same flexibility to an unknown disabled actor trying to get through the audition-room door.

Esmond Knight provides perhaps an even more extraordinary example. Already an established actor, Knight served aboard HMS Prince of Wales during the Second World War and was severely injured during the battle involving the Bismarck. He lost his sight, remaining totally blind for roughly two years before recovering limited vision in one eye.

After WW2 he returned to acting.

Knight went on to work repeatedly with Michael Powell and Emeric Pressburger, appearing in films including Black Narcissus and The Red Shoes, as well as continuing a substantial theatrical career. His visual impairment did not prevent him from performing Shakespeare, working on complex film sets or sustaining a career across decades. Knight's career dismantles an argument still sometimes heard in casting discussions today: that disabled performers inevitably create insurmountable problems of blocking, continuity, movement, safety or production logistics.

Directors adapted.

Knight adapted.

The work got made.

Jack Elam offers a different kind of history. Blind in his left eye following a childhood injury, Elam's distinctive gaze became one of the most recognisable faces in American westerns. He built a career lasting decades, initially specialising in villains before increasingly turning his screen persona towards comedy. His impairment was visible without requiring each character to carry a disability narrative. Turner Classic Movies records that deteriorating eyesight forced him out of his earlier career in accounting before he moved into acting. This is significant because Elam's screen presence offers something still surprisingly rare: disability existing as part of an actor's physical identity without necessarily becoming the subject of the story.

Other careers reveal different sides of the same history.

Rondo Hatton, whose appearance changed as a result of acromegaly, found regular work but was repeatedly channelled into threatening and monstrous roles. Michael Dunn, an actor with dwarfism of considerable dramatic range, broke through those limitations far enough to receive an Academy Award nomination for Ship of Fools, yet still encountered an industry offering a restricted range of parts. Harold Russell, who lost both hands during military service, gave one of the landmark authentic disability performances in The Best Years of Our Lives and won both a competitive supporting actor Oscar and a special Academy Award for the same performance.

Montgomery Clift continued acting after the 1956 car crash that severely altered his face and left him living with chronic pain. Howard McNear returned to The Andy Griffith Show after a stroke affected his mobility, with production adapting how his character was staged. These examples are different in circumstance and should not be collapsed into a single disability identity, but together they reveal something important: film and television have always known how to accommodate bodily change when they decide a performer is worth keeping. Clift continued making significant work after his accident, including Wild River and Judgment at Nuremberg.

The recurring barrier, then, is not creative impossibility. It is who Hollywood decides is worth adapting for.

### Time Bandits and the possibility of being the adventure

By 1981, Terry Gilliam's Time Bandits offered another important shift.

Actors with dwarfism including David Rappaport, Kenny Baker, Malcolm Dixon, Mike Edmonds, Jack Purvis and Tiny Ross were not simply assembled to decorate a fantasy world. They were the unruly ensemble driving the adventure. They argued, schemed, stole, panicked, failed, joked and propelled the story forward.

That distinction matters.

Kiran Shah, whose own extraordinary career has included acting, stunt work and body doubling across major productions, later described Gilliam's film as changing the perspective on performers with dwarfism. Shah had originally been cast in material that was cut, but he has pointed to Time Bandits as a moment when actors such as Kenny Baker and Malcolm Dixon were given genuinely prominent dramatic roles rather than simply being treated as spectacle.

Even here, fantasy remained the doorway.

That has been one of the persistent paradoxes for actors of short stature. Hollywood has often been willing to believe they can be warriors, goblins, aliens, magical beings and inhabitants of impossible universes before believing they can simply be a person who happens to work in an office.

Warwick Davis built an extraordinary career beginning with Star Wars, moving through Willow, Harry Potter and many other productions. Kiran Shah developed a remarkable craft career as a performer, stunt specialist and scale double, working across Superman, Star Wars, Raiders of the Lost Ark, Titanic and The Lord of the Rings. Their careers demonstrate enormous technical and performance skill. They also show how frequently that skill has been channelled through fantasy, prosthetics, creature work or body doubling rather than ordinary human roles.

The talent was always there. The imaginative range of the casting was narrower.

### From Marlee Matlin to a generation reclaiming the screen

When Marlee Matlin won the Academy Award for Children of a Lesser God in 1987, she became the first Deaf performer to win an acting Oscar, doing so for her first feature-film performance. It was a historic achievement, but what followed was equally significant: she continued working.

Matlin built a long career across film and television rather than remaining an isolated example attached to one "important" disability drama. Decades later, CODA placed Deaf performers including Matlin, Troy Kotsur and Daniel Durant at the centre of a family story in which Deaf culture shaped the narrative without reducing its characters to medical case studies. Kotsur became the second Deaf performer to win an acting Oscar in 2022.

Peter Dinklage represents another major shift. His importance is not simply that he became a successful actor with dwarfism, but that performances such as Finbar McBride in The Station Agent demonstrated how disability could be simultaneously present and not the entire plot. Finbar's body affects how strangers perceive him. It affects social encounters and the assumptions made about him. The film does not pretend difference is socially meaningless.

But he is not a diagnosis disguised as a character.

He is allowed solitude, irritation, attraction, friendship, sexuality, humour and contradiction.

That is far closer to how incidental disability can work: the actor's lived physicality remains visible and may influence the world around the character, but the character exists far beyond it.

Kathryn Hunter offers another challenge to conventional ideas about the "correct" body for leading roles. After sustaining serious injuries as a young actor, she developed a performance vocabulary in which an unconventional physicality became part of an extraordinary theatrical and screen craft rather than something to be normalised or concealed. Her career has encompassed Shakespearean kings, experimental theatre and major films.

These performers belong to a much larger contemporary generation now pushing beyond the idea that inclusion means occasionally casting a disabled actor in a disability story.

The next frontier is far more ambitious. It is the right to play characters whose disability was never specified at all.

### Hollywood loves disability stories more than it loves disabled actors

This may be the central contradiction in the entire history. There is no shortage of disability in film.

There are hundreds of examples of disabled characters across cinema and television. A running IMDb research compilation cited in disability-casting work contains more than 500 examples of non-disabled actors cast in disabled roles, while broader databases document many more cases across film and television. The exact historical total depends on definitions and incomplete records but the scale of the pattern is beyond serious dispute.

The Academy Awards reveal the same fascination.

A widely cited analysis published in 2020 identified 61 acting nominations for performances as disabled characters, producing 27 wins. At that point, only two of those winning performers were themselves disabled: Harold Russell and Marlee Matlin. Troy Kotsur has since joined that exceptionally small group. Different datasets use different definitions of disability, so precise totals vary, but the historical imbalance is unmistakable.

Hollywood has therefore never suffered from a lack of interest in disability as dramatic material. Quite the opposite.

Disability is considered interesting.

Transformation is interesting.

Difference is interesting.

Dependency, adaptation, prejudice, bodily change, communication and altered relationships with the world all generate drama.

The problem is that the industry has frequently wanted the story without the actor. A non-disabled performer temporarily acquiring a disability can be presented as transformation. Makeup, prosthetics, movement coaching and imitation become visible evidence of acting. The audience can admire how completely the performer has changed.

Meanwhile, an actor who actually arrives with that embodiment can struggle even to get an audition.

The same characteristic that is considered award-worthy when simulated can be treated as a casting obstacle when real.

That is one of Hollywood's strangest creative contradictions.

### The missing revolution: incidental disability

Authentic casting of explicitly disabled characters matters, but it solves only half the problem.

The more profound change will come when disabled actors are routinely considered for roles in which disability is not specified.

A judge. A spy. A parent. A corrupt politician. A romantic lead. A schoolteacher. A serial killer. A superhero. A friend drinking coffee, a croissant and a chat in a scene. This is where representation has historically remained extraordinarily thin.

Recent data shows how persistent the imbalance is. A 2025 Ruderman Family Foundation and Geena Davis Institute analysis of 350 scripted television series from 2016 to 2023 found disability representation at only 3.9%, with nearly 80% of disabled characters inauthentically cast. In the top 100 films of 2024, USC Annenberg research found that just 2.4% of speaking or named characters were depicted with a disability.

These numbers expose why authentic casting alone cannot be the end goal. Suppose every explicitly disabled character were authentically cast tomorrow. That would be progress, but disabled actors would still be competing for a tiny, predetermined category of roles.

Equality means something larger. It means allowing disability to enter a production through the actor.

Jack Elam did not need every screenplay rewritten to explain his eye.

Lionel Barrymore's wheelchair did not prevent Mr Potter from owning half of Bedford Falls.

Peter Dinklage does not need every character he plays to possess an elaborate medical origin story.

Sometimes the interesting creative choice is simply to cast the actor.

Then let the world of the story adjust.

### The excuses no longer survive contact with history

One of the strangest features of the debate around disability casting is how consistently the same explanations return.

#### **1\. The first argument is that there are not enough talented disabled actors.**

A century of film history has already answered that, and if you don't believe that there are lists such as this one on IMDB.

Granville Redmond, Emerson Romero, Lionel Barrymore, Esmond Knight, Jack Elam, Harold Russell, Michael Dunn, Marlee Matlin, Warwick Davis, Peter Dinklage, Kathryn Hunter, Kiran Shah and generations around them did not succeed because standards were lowered. They succeeded because they were good at their jobs.

#### **2\. The second argument is that there are not enough roles.**

Again, the evidence says the opposite.

If hundreds of disabled characters can be identified as having been played by non-disabled actors, then the industry plainly has no shortage of disabled roles. The Marvel and DC universes alone contain a remarkable density of characters with blindness, Deafness, limb difference, paralysis, disfigurement, chronic conditions, prosthetics and cybernetic adaptations. Research into those franchises has found a striking gap between the abundance of disability in the source material and the comparatively small number of disabled performers cast to embody it.

There are scripts.

There are actors.

The missing element is imagination.

#### **3\. The third argument is that "any actor should be able to play anything" and that the role should simply go to "the best actor for the job."**

That principle sounds neutral until we examine who has historically been allowed to benefit from it. If non-disabled actors can compete for disabled characters, while visibly disabled actors are rarely considered for characters written without disability, the system is not open casting.

It is one-way casting.

The "best actor" can only emerge from the people permitted into the room. To people accustomed to unrestricted access to almost every category of role, equity can sometimes feel like a new restriction. But expanding opportunity is not the destruction of artistic freedom. It is the removal of an old monopoly over who has been allowed to exercise it.

#### **4\. The final argument is the demand for perfect authenticity: the idea that unless an actor shares a character's exact diagnosis, impairment, history and bodily presentation, authentic casting becomes impossible.**

That creates a false choice between perfect biological matching and complete inauthenticity. Casting has always involved interpretation.

A Deaf signing character may require a performer with the relevant linguistic and cultural experience because those qualities are fundamental to the role. A wheelchair user's movement and relationship with space may be similarly central. In other cases, a performer with different but relevant lived experience may bring more authenticity and creative insight than an attempt at diagnostic exactitude.

The goal is not a genetic matching exercise. It is to stop treating non-disabled embodiment as the automatic default.

### What the pioneers actually proved

The history of disabled actors is sometimes told as a sequence of firsts.

The first Deaf star.

The first wheelchair user in a major role.

The first disabled Oscar winner.

The first actor of short stature to break out of fantasy.

Those milestones matter, but concentrating only on firsts can obscure the deeper story.

These performers were creative pioneers.

The Deaf actors of silent cinema demonstrated how richly visual performance could communicate without spoken language. Granville Redmond's world intersected with Chaplin's at precisely the moment cinema was inventing its physical grammar. Emerson Romero moved from performing in an accessible visual medium to helping invent ways of making its sound-era successor accessible again.

The actors in Freaks demonstrated the dramatic power of a disabled ensemble and community almost a century before Hollywood began talking seriously about authentic representation.

The performers of The Wizard of Oz showed that audiences would embrace visibly different bodies in one of cinema's most enduring popular films, even as the industry failed to offer them an equivalent range of roles afterwards.

Lionel Barrymore demonstrated that a wheelchair did not diminish authority, menace or star power.

Esmond Knight proved that major physical and sensory change did not end an actor's capacity for sophisticated work.

Jack Elam showed that a visibly different face could become an unforgettable screen presence without requiring a medical explanation.

Harold Russell demonstrated the emotional detail an actor could bring from lived experience.

Michael Dunn pushed against the assumption that actors with dwarfism belonged only in spectacle and fantasy.

Time Bandits allowed a group of actors with dwarfism to become the adventure rather than decorate it.

Marlee Matlin proved that Deaf performance belonged at the highest levels of mainstream screen acting.

Warwick Davis and Kiran Shah demonstrated craft across acting, physical performance, stunt work and some of cinema's largest technical productions.

Peter Dinklage helped make it increasingly difficult to argue that an actor with dwarfism could not be a complicated lead whose body mattered without becoming the sole reason for the story.

Kathryn Hunter demonstrates something equally important: that physical difference can become part of an actor's expressive vocabulary rather than an obstacle to be corrected.

Each generation has expanded the creative possibilities available to the next. And yet each generation has been asked to prove itself again. That may be the most frustrating lesson in this entire history.

After more than a century, the question is no longer whether disabled actors can carry films, play complex characters, perform demanding physical work, attract audiences, win major awards or enrich stories through lived experience.

**All of that has already been demonstrated.**

**Repeatedly.**

The real question is why an industry built upon imagination has remained so unimaginative about where disabled actors belong.

The next great step will not be another single breakthrough performance. It will be when "breakthrough" is no longer necessary.

· When a Deaf actor can play a character whose Deafness is incidental.

· When an amputee actor can be cast without the screenplay needing an accident scene.

· When a wheelchair user can enter a romantic comedy without the chair becoming the plot.

· When an actor of short stature can play the politician, murderer, parent, professor or romantic lead without being required to inhabit a fantasy species.

· When a visibly disabled actor can simply appear because they gave the most interesting audition.

The pioneers did not prove that disabled people could act. They proved that disability could expand the language of performance.

Hollywood's history of disabled acting is therefore not a history of absence. It is a history of presence repeatedly treated as exception.

The actors were always there. The imagination needed to see everything they could be is what has taken more than a century to begin catching-up.
