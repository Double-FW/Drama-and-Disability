# Incidental Disability Casting: Why Directors Should Treat the Incidental Visibility of Disability as a Creative Opportunity

When we talk about disability on screen or stage, the conversation too often stalls on "worthy" narratives. Disability becomes the focus of a drama about suffering, tragedy, or inspiration, something to be explained, pitied, or overcome. But what if we've been looking at this all wrong? What if disability isn't just a subject, but one of the richest creative opportunities a director has at their disposal?

## Beyond "Roles About Disability"

Casting disabled actors only when a script demands it is like casting people of colour only when race is written in. It reduces identity to a plot point. Yet in real life, disability cuts across every role imaginable: parents, lovers, detectives, heroes, villains, teachers, neighbours, kings, and fools.

The vast majority of scripted characters , by some estimates over 90%, could plausibly be played by a disabled actor without changing a word of dialogue. That's the essence of incidental portrayal: disability present but not foregrounded. Authenticity doesn't require rewriting the story, it requires rethinking who we imagine in the role.

## Why Incidental Casting is Artistically Exciting for the Audience

_"Creativity is about making interesting choices, and disability can be an interesting choice"_ [_Zak Ford-Williams_](https://www.imdb.com/name/nm12923816)_, Actor_

1. **New dimensions of character**  
   When a character walks into a room with a cane, or signs instead of speaks, it instantly reshapes how we perceive them. Their vulnerabilities, resilience, and resourcefulness emerge before a single line is spoken.
2. **Shifts in dynamics**  
   Casting a disabled actor alters how others interact with them, often unconsciously. A pause, a glance, a hesitation to offer help. These micro-moments, unscripted but real, add texture to scenes and reveal truths about characters around them.
3. **Breaking cliché**  
   Hollywood and West End alike are addicted to polished, able-bodied "perfection." Incidental casting breaks that monotony. It tells audiences: heroes, villains, mentors, lovers, they all exist in disabled bodies too.
4. **Audience engagement**  
   Viewers notice. They lean in. When disability isn't "the story," but simply present, it subverts expectation and forces audiences to confront their own assumptions. Far from distracting, it deepens immersion.

## Practical Tools for Directors

- **Ask the question early**: At the start of casting, take each role and ask, "Why couldn't a disabled actor play this?"
- **Bring in a Creative Disability Consultant**: They don't write your script; they help you see possibilities you may have missed.
- **Focus on lived experience, not labels**: Disability doesn't need to be named. What matters is the authenticity an actor brings to the role.
- **Resist the urge to explain**: Don't feel obliged to write in "the accident" or "the diagnosis." Let disability exist without backstory.

## Examples of Incidental Disability Casting

### Lord Remington — [**Bridgerton, Series 3**](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt27513166/?ref_=ttep_ep_2) **(Zak Ford-Williams)**

A standout example of pure incidental casting: Lord Remington, a romantic interest, uses a wheelchair. His disability is never mentioned, explained, or central to the plot. He is simply part of the romantic swirl of the Bridgerton universe. This is perhaps the best recent case of disability being present without being narrativised — normalised, elegant, and unforced.

### Walter Jr. — [Breaking Bad](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt0903747) (RJ Mitte)

A teenage son with cerebral palsy, played by an actor with the same condition. His disability is part of his life, but the show never sensationalises it — he's a normal teenager navigating family chaos. A landmark in authentic casting.

### James — [Three Billboards Outside Ebbing, Missouri](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt5027774) (Peter Dinklage)

James is a love interest and outsider, played with subtlety. His disability is never the punchline, but part of how others perceive him, which adds poignancy to his interactions and gives depth to an otherwise minor role.

### Butchie — [The Wire](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt0306414/) (S. Robert Morgan)

A blind bar owner who becomes a trusted confidant. His blindness adds mystery and gravity, making him memorable in a cast full of vivid characters. Disability here enriches texture without driving the narrative.

### Columbo — [Columbo](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt1466074/) (Peter Falk)

Peter Falk's glass eye, never mentioned in the series, became inseparable from Columbo's look. His "askew" gaze made him distinctively offbeat — turning what could have been a gimmicky detective into an iconic character.

### Maker Keeper — [Dune: Part Two](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt15239678) (Alison Halstead)

In her scene with the baby sandworm, Halstead's physicality creates an aura of ritual and spirituality. Her presence demonstrates how embodied difference can elevate fantastical world-building.

### Gary — [Joker](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt7286456/) (Leigh Gill)

Gary, a little person, is trapped with Arthur during one of the film's most violent moments. His size and perceived vulnerability heighten audience tension, but it's his kindness and decency that save him — a layered twist on expectations.

### Owen Davies — [Better](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt14466190/) (Zak Ford-Williams)

One of the only examples of a disabled actor portraying a progressive condition. Ford-Williams, who has cerebral palsy, used his lived experience to portray masking and unmasking his impairment, pushing the boundaries of authentic casting.

### Domitius — I, [Claudius](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt0074006/) (Esmond Knight)

Not scripted as blind, but Knight, who lost his sight in WWII, played him convincingly. A reminder that unless explicitly written, most characters could be cast as either disabled or non-disabled.

### Bishop de Quadra — [Elizabeth R](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt0066652/) (Esmond Knight)

Again, Knight's blindness was never in the script. But in a historical context where impairments were more common, his presence added authenticity and richness to the drama.

### Makkari — [Eternals](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt9032400/) (Lauren Ridloff)

A speedster superhero who is Deaf, played by a Deaf actress. Her deafness becomes part of her power dynamic and team interactions, proving incidental disability casting works even in blockbuster Marvel storytelling.

### Connie — [The Walking Dead](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt1520211/) (Lauren Ridloff)

Ridloff again brings her Deafness into the apocalyptic horror. Connie communicates through sign language, enriching both the survival dynamics and the group's diversity of communication.

### Clarissa Mullery — [Silent Witness](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt0115355/) (Liz Carr)

A forensic examiner and recurring character. Carr, a wheelchair user, isn't defined by disability — she's witty, sharp, and essential to the team. A great case of incidental integration in a long-running drama.

### Dianne — [Doctor Who](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt0436992/) (Nadia Albina)

Introduced as a love interest, Albina has a limb difference that is never mentioned in dialogue. This is textbook incidental casting: disability is visible, unremarked, and simply part of the world.

### The Witches — [The Tragedy of Macbeth](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt10095582) (Kathryn Hunter)

Hunter's unique physicality made her portrayal uncanny and otherworldly. She demonstrated how lived bodily difference can bring an entirely new physical vocabulary to classic roles.

### Roman Lunyov — [I Care A Lot](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt9893250) (Peter Dinklage)

A ruthless gangster kingpin. Dinklage's stature complicates audience assumptions about both his authority and ability.

### Mr Potter — [It's a Wonderful Life](https://www.imdb.com/video/vi1101988121/) (Lionel Barrymore)

Barrymore, a wheelchair user, played Potter without the disability ever being scripted. His stillness amplified the character's coldness and authority, proving incidental presence has powerful creative effects.

### Cherry Dorrington — [Harlots](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt5761478) (Francesca Mills)

A brothel worker navigating power and survival. Mills' short stature adds complexity to the show's class and gender politics without reducing her to stereotype.

## Why This List Matters

These examples prove that incidental disability portrayal:

- **works across genres** — romance, sci-fi, drama, action, fantasy.
- **strengthens characterisation** — adding nuance without needing new exposition.
- **normalises diversity** — audiences accept it naturally when disability is part of the world-building.

For casting directors, the message is clear: you don't need to wait for a "disabled role" to cast disabled talent. Look at any script and ask: _why not here?_

## Creative Courage

Incidental casting is not a risk. It is an act of artistic bravery. It takes the world as it is, diverse, messy, full of different bodies and minds, and weaves that reality into our stories without apology.

Directors often pride themselves on making bold creative choices. This is one of the boldest and simplest available: stop waiting for a script to require disability and start seeing disability as an untapped reservoir of nuance, tension, and truth.

The next great performance might not come from an actor despite their disability, but because of the lived experience they bring to a role you hadn't thought to offer them.

# The Incidental Casting Manifesto: 7 Creative Opportunities

1. **Flip the Default**  
   Instead of assuming characters are non-disabled unless stated otherwise, ask: "Could this role be played by a disabled actor?"
2. **Prioritise Authenticity Over Exposition**  
   Disability doesn't need a backstory. Trust audiences to accept it without explanation.
3. **See Disability as Subtext**  
   A cane, a prosthetic, or a signing character can add silent layers of meaning to a scene without a single word changed.
4. **Cast for Humanity, Not Diagnosis**  
   Focus on what the actor brings; their perspective, timing, vulnerability, power, not whether their impairment matches a scripted condition.
5. **Normalise, Don't Tokenise**  
   Make disability part of the everyday fabric of your stories. It doesn't have to be highlighted, only present.
6. **Consult, Don't Assume**  
   Use Creative Disability Consultants to explore possibilities. Their role is to expand your imagination, not restrict it.
7. **Be Brave, Not Box-Ticking**  
   Treat incidental casting as a chance to disrupt clichés, spark new dynamics, surprise your audience and be interesting.
