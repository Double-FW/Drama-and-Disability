# "No Cure at the Curtain Call": Reclaiming Joseph Merrick through Authentic Portrayal

For more than a century, [Joseph Merrick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Merrick), wrongly remembered as "John", has stood as one of the most mythologised figures in British theatrical and cinematic history. Better known in popular culture as the "Elephant Man," Merrick's life has repeatedly been appropriated for dramatic effect, often through the lens of pity, deformity, otherness or redemptive suffering.

His story has been adapted by celebrated writers and filmmakers, yet few have asked who should tell it, or more crucially, who gets to be Joseph on screen or stage.

## The Fiction of Treves

The most dominant narrative of [Merrick's life](https://www.nineteenthcenturydisability.org/items/show/38) was penned not by him, but by Frederick Treves, a surgeon who encountered Merrick at the London Hospital. Treves's 1923 memoir wrongly named him "John," recasting Merrick as a voiceless, saintly victim redeemed by medical charity, a framing that conveniently placed Treves as the compassionate hero and narrator of Merrick's transformation.

That version stuck. It inspired David Lynch's 1980 film [The Elephant Man](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt0080678/) and Bernard Pomerance's 1977 stage play of the same name. In both, Merrick was performed by non-disabled actors, such as David Bowie on stage and John Hurt on screen, who used prosthetics and physically contorting themselves to suggest Merrick's condition, but without any lived experience to inform the portrayal. What followed is not simply performance, but what critics now call "[cripping-up](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cripping-up)", the modern theatrical equivalent of blackface for disability.

## The Real and Imagined History

In 2023, a new production from [Nottingham Playhouse](https://nottinghamplayhouse.co.uk/events/the-real-imagined-history-of-the-elephant-man/) and [The Royal Theatrical Support Trust](http://rtst.org.uk) brought Joseph Merrick back to life, and back to himself.

Tom Wright's [The Real and Imagined History of the Elephant Man](https://www.nickhernbooks.co.uk/the-real-imagined-history-of-the-elephant-man) set out to dismantle the Treves myth. It places Merrick at the centre, rehumanised as witty, angry, imaginative, kind, trusting and articulate. The play goes deeper into his actual life, dreams, and personhood, while also confronting the disturbing reality that Merrick's skeleton is still displayed in a glass case at Queen Mary University of London, turning him into an object of spectacle even in death.

This haunting fact became a central visual device in director Stephen Bailey's staging, where an illuminated glass case looms throughout the play, a reminder that he never truly escaped being a spectacle to be displayed and put on show.

## Authenticity on Stage: Zak Ford-Williams and Daniel Monks

This retelling became profoundly political when Merrick was played not by a non-disabled actor, but by [Zak Ford-Williams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zak_Ford-Williams), a disabled actor with quadriplegic cerebral palsy. In earlier productions of the same play in Australia, Merrick had been played by [Daniel Monks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Monks), who is also disabled.

Both actors brought their lived experiences to the role that has historically been performed at disabled people rather than by them.

_"Playing Joseph wasn't about pretending to be someone deformed or grotesque. It was about allowing the truth of my own body to stand in for his. That's something a non-disabled actor can never do honestly."_  
[Daniel Monks, Arts Review 2017](https://artsreview.com.au/the-real-and-imagined-history-of-the-elephant-man/)

Zak Ford-Williams echoed that sense of grounded authenticity in multiple interviews. Speaking to Able Magazine, he said:

_"I think there's a slight advantage, because I'm so used to being very aware and having to control my body and my mouth. When I have to change my physicality or my voice I have, I feel, a great awareness to begin with."_  
Zak Ford-Williams, [Able Magazine, Sept 2023](https://ablemagazine.co.uk/interview-zak-ford-williams/)

And in a Blackpool Social Club feature:

_"We rarely get the chance to explore disabled people's inner lives on stage. This play doesn't make Joseph into a metaphor, it lets him be a person."_  
Zak Ford-Williams, [Blackpool Social Club, 2023](https://www.blackpoolsocial.club/41826-righting-historic-wrongs-zak-ford-williams-on-playing-the-elephant-man/)

## "No Cure at the Curtain Call"

There is a moment of profound rupture when a visibly disabled actor plays a disabled character: the boundary between character and actor doesn't neatly vanish at curtain call. The audience can't comfort themselves with the illusion that the actor will "return to normal" once the performance ends. As Dr. Jessie Parrott, a leading scholar in disability and casting, puts it in their 2019 PhD thesis:

_"There is no 'cure at the curtain call'."_  
Jessica Parrott, [WRAP Thesis, University of Warwick](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/153265/)

This simple truth destabilises the theatrical logic of cripping-up, which relies on transformation, the actor's triumph over the body. With disabled actors, the body isn't an obstacle to overcome. It's the medium of truth.

Ford-Williams also reflected on this shift in the relationship between the character and the audience.

_"Putting a disabled person on a stage or screen is a postmodernist act in itself."  
Zak Ford-Williams_

## The BBC Controversy: A Missed Moment

This makes the [BBC's abandoned 2019 Elephant Man project](https://pagesix.com/2018/08/30/bbc-faces-backlash-for-casting-charlie-heaton-in-elephant-man/) feel even more regressive in hindsight. Initially announced with [Charlie Heaton](https://www.imdb.com/name/nm3492710/) (a non-disabled actor from Stranger Things) in the title role, the project drew immediate backlash from disabled artists and activists.

As [Sky News reported](https://news.sky.com/story/anger-over-casting-of-stranger-things-star-charlie-heaton-as-elephant-man-11485440), the move was seen as another example of cripping-up, a decision made despite the growing pool of talented disabled actors with both classical training and screen experience.

Among those voicing concern was actor and activist [Melissa Johns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melissa_Johns), who said:

_"We are still seen as risks. We are not trusted to tell our own stories."_

The BBC never officially acknowledged the criticism, but the project quietly vanished from schedules and press releases. Subsequently the announcement in May 2025 of [Adam Pearson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_Pearson_%28actor%29) to play Joseph in the first screen adaptation of the original Bernard Pomerance. Adam has reflected on the significance of this project:

_"Joseph Merrick is a man I have a long and complex relationship with. From having his name used as a term of derision to learning about him in documentaries I've presented, it's been a cathartic journey of growing to love and respect a man who as a child I avoided even thinking about. Now as a disability advocate and actor, I can think of no greater honour (yet heavy responsibility) than to tell the true story of Joseph Carey Merrick"  
Adam Pearson,_ [_The Guardian, 2025_](https://www.theguardian.com/film/2025/may/01/a-different-man-adam-pearson-to-star-in-new-film-of-the-elephant-man)

## Legacy in Bones and Words

What makes [The Real and Imagined History of the Elephant Man](https://www.nickhernbooks.co.uk/the-real-imagined-history-of-the-elephant-man) especially haunting is that its title is true: there is a real history which is distorted, buried, incomplete, and an imagined one, fuelled by able-bodied sentimentality and cinematic fantasy. And Joseph Merrick is still not free. His bones remain on display. His name is still widely misremembered. But with productions like Bailey's and performances like Ford-Williams's, a counter-narrative is emerging, one that doesn't just retell Merrick's story, but reclaims it.
