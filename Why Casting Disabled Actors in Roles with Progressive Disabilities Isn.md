# Why Casting Disabled Actors in Roles with Progressive Disabilities Isn't Just Possible, It's Essential

When it comes to casting characters with progressive disabilities, a persistent argument in film and TV circles is that only non-disabled actors can convincingly portray the evolving nature of these conditions. [Eddie Redmayne](https://www.imdb.com/name/nm1519666/)'s acclaimed performance as Stephen Hawking in [The Theory of Everything](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt2980516) often comes up as the "proof" of this idea.

But is this really true? Or is it an excuse that keeps disabled actors sidelined from the kinds of roles they deserve?

**Enter** [**Zak Ford-Williams**](https://www.imdb.com/name/nm12923816/)**.** A disabled actor with quadraplegic cerebral palsy, Zak took a leading role in the [BBC drama Better (2022)](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt14466190), delivering a nuanced, authentic performance that challenges the industry's narrow casting assumptions. His work offers a powerful counterpoint to the argument that progressive disability roles require non-disabled actors.

"Disability is not just a physical condition — it's an experience, an identity, and a way of being in the world. As actors, we bring all of that with us to a role, no matter the character's story." [Zak Ford-Williams, Actors and Performers Yearbook, 2024](https://actorsandperformers.com/advice-for-disabled-actors-from-zak-ford-williams-actors-performers-yearbook-2024/)

## The Flawed Logic Behind the "Progression" Argument

The reasoning goes: progressive disabilities change a character's physicality, speech, or abilities over time, and disabled actors with stable conditions can't realistically "act the progression." So, non-disabled actors supposedly have the flexibility to portray this change better.

This logic is flawed for several reasons:

- **Acting is transformation.** Actors routinely portray people very different from themselves — whether in age, background, or ability. Why exclude disabled actors from this fundamental part of the craft?
- **Lived experience informs authenticity.** Disabled actors bring unique insights into living with disability, which adds depth and honesty to performances.
- **Progression isn't just physical.** It's emotional, social, and psychological — areas where disabled actors excel in conveying truth and complexity.

As scholar and disability activist Liz Carr notes:

"Authentic representation means letting disabled actors tell our stories, not through imitation but through lived experience combined with craft." — Liz Carr, Disability Advocate and Actress

## Zak Ford-Williams and Better: Breaking New Ground

Zak's performance in Better is more than a role; it's a statement. Despite living with cerebral palsy, Zak portrays a character with multifaceted challenges and changing circumstances. The show doesn't shy away from the realities of disability, it embraces them.

His presence on screen shatters misconceptions that disabled actors can't handle complex or evolving roles. More importantly, it expands what we consider possible in disability representation.

"I want to see disabled actors playing all kinds of roles — not just the 'disability story' but the whole human experience." Zak Ford-Williams

## Why Casting Disabled Actors Matters

Representation is more than visibility, it's about opportunity, dignity, and inclusion.

- Disabled actors deserve access to the full range of roles, not just those explicitly written as disabled or stable conditions.
- Denying them progressive roles perpetuates industry ableism and limits storytelling.
- Authentic casting invites audiences to engage with disability beyond stereotypes.

The UK's Creative Diversity Network recommends:

"Authentic casting of disabled actors leads to more truthful storytelling and wider cultural understanding." Creative Diversity Network, _Disability in TV and Film_, 2023

## Moving Forward: Recommendations for Industry Professionals

If you're a producer, casting director, or writer, here's what you can do:

1. **Cast disabled actors in all disability roles, including progressive ones.**Provide coaching and support as you would for any challenging role.
2. **Engage disabled talent early in development.** Their insights enrich the story and character development.
3. **Reject assumptions about "matching" disabilities.** Focus on performance, authenticity, and respect.
4. **Collaborate with disability consultants.** Ensure portrayals are accurate and sensitive.

### Conclusion

Zak Ford-Williams' role in Better reminds us that the acting craft, transformation, empathy, and storytelling, transcends disability labels. It's time to discard outdated casting assumptions and open doors for disabled actors in every role, including those depicting progressive disabilities.

After all, authentic storytelling demands nothing less.
