# British TV Says It Wants Authentic Disability on Screen. But Is It Measuring the Right Thing?

_The UK television industry has become much better at counting disabled people. The harder question is whether it can tell us what audiences are actually seeing._

## The British television industry has no shortage of disability commitments

The BBC talks about authentic and incidental portrayal. Channel 4 wants disabled people to appear in stories that are incidental, integrated and disability-led. Sky has set a target for 10% of people appearing on screen to be perceived as disabled. ITV has invested in programmes involving Deaf, disabled and neurodivergent talent and stories. UKTV requires productions to meet a 15% Deaf, disabled or neurodivergent representation target.

Behind these commitments sits the [Creative Diversity Network](https://www.creativediversitynetwork.com/), or CDN, whose members include the BBC, ITV, Channel 4, Paramount, Sky, UKTV and Warner Bros. Discovery. CDN also manages Diamond, the television industry's shared diversity-monitoring system. Diamond has done something important. It has given broadcasters a common way to collect information about who works in television, on and off screen. It has helped expose the exclusion of disabled people from the industry and supported initiatives such as [Doubling Disability](https://www.creativediversitynetwork.com/doubling-disability) and the [TV Access Project](https://www.bbc.co.uk/creativediversity/i-hub/home/tv-access-project).

But there is a problem. Counting disabled people is not the same as measuring disability portrayal.

That distinction matters because broadcasters are no longer promising only to employ more disabled people. They are also making creative commitments: to cast disabled actors in disabled roles, consider them for characters not written as disabled and create fictional worlds in which disability can be present without always becoming the story.

Can Diamond tell us whether those commitments are being delivered?

At present, not reliably.

## Representation and portrayal are different questions

Diamond collects two main kinds of information.

Its **"Actual"** data is based on information contributors provide about themselves. If an actor identifies as disabled, their work contributes to the disability representation figure whether or not an audience would know they were disabled from watching the programme.

Its **"Perceived"** data concerns how a character or contributor might be understood on screen. For drama, the production company watches the programme and records characteristics it believes an audience may perceive in the character.

These measures answer different questions.

**Actual data asks:** are disabled people getting work?

**Perceived data asks:** might audiences understand these people or characters to be disabled?

Neither measure, on its own, tells us whether a disabled character was played by a disabled actor. Nor does it identify when a disabled performer was cast in an ordinary role and allowed to bring their own embodiment into the fictional world.

This creates several possible outcomes.

1. A disabled actor with a non-apparent condition may play a character whose disability is never communicated. That is disability representation, but not necessarily disability portrayal.

2\. A non-disabled actor may play a character explicitly written as disabled. That creates disability portrayal, but it is not authentic casting.

3\. A disabled actor may play a disabled character with relevant lived experience. That provides both representation and authentic portrayal.

4\. A disabled actor may also be cast as a solicitor, detective, parent, neighbour or political adviser whose disability was never specified in the script. If the actor's disability is apparent, their presence becomes part of the world of the programme without needing to become a plot. That is incidental portrayal, and it may be one of the most creatively significant outcomes of all.

A single headline percentage cannot distinguish between these possibilities.

### Who decides what the audience perceives?

Diamond's Perceived data is entered by production companies reviewing their own programmes. The current CDN guidance describes this as the submitter's perception and says there are no right or wrong answers.

That is honest about the subjectivity involved, but it raises an obvious question: whose perception is being measured?

A producer cannot stand in for every viewer. Disabled audiences may recognise embodiment, communication or assistive technology that other viewers overlook. Someone familiar with a performer may perceive disability immediately, while a first-time viewer may not. Costume, framing, editing and screen time can make disability more or less apparent.

There is also no published requirement that disabled people participate in making or reviewing these classifications.

This is where **"Nothing About Us Without Us"** becomes more than an industry slogan. If disability portrayal is being measured, disabled people should help define the categories, apply them and resolve uncertain cases. Their involvement is not only an ethical safeguard. It improves the quality of the research.

Nor is guidance alone enough to demonstrate consistency. Would two people classify the same performance in the same way? Would a disabled assessor and a non-disabled assessor notice the same things? What happens when they disagree?

Without testing agreement between assessors, the size of the possible error remains unknown.

### The risk of missing incidental casting

CDN is redesigning Diamond. Its current guidance says production companies will enter Perceived information for "eligible main characters and contributors." There may be practical reasons for focusing on major roles. Television productions can contain hundreds of credited and uncredited contributors, and recording every appearance creates a substantial administrative burden.

But this choice may produce a serious blind spot.

Incidental inclusion is not confined to protagonists. It can be found among supporting characters, guests, day players, stunt performers, walk-ons and background artists. These are the people who make a hospital, police station, office, street or fantasy kingdom feel inhabited. If Diamond concentrates on main characters, it may overlook the very casting choices that reveal whether disability has become an ordinary part of the television landscape.

That presents an interesting creative question.

**What does inclusion look like when disability is not driving the plot?**

Is it the wheelchair-using barrister whose chair is never explained? The Deaf shopkeeper who communicates naturally with a customer? The actor with a limb difference playing a starship commander without an origin story about their body? The person with short stature who appears in a crowd without being transformed into a visual joke or fantasy creature?

These choices may occupy little screen time, but collectively they determine whose bodies appear to belong in the imagined world.

## Broadcasters are promising different things

Diamond's usefulness also varies because CDN members have not made identical commitments.

The BBC now distinguishes between "landmark" and "incidental" portrayal in its creative-diversity reporting. Landmark portrayal places an underrepresented story or subject at the centre of a programme. Incidental portrayal includes people from underrepresented backgrounds without making identity the focus.

Diamond can tell the BBC something about disabled contributors and producer-coded characters. But without linking the performer, character and role, it cannot show how many disabled actors were cast incidentally or whether explicitly disabled roles were cast authentically.

Channel 4's Disability Code of Portrayal goes further. It asks productions to give disabled people agency, use the Social Model of Disability, remove the "able gaze" and treat disability as a valid identity. It distinguishes incidental, integrated and core portrayal and encourages the casting of disabled performers both in disabled roles and in roles not written as disabled.

No numerical count can establish whether a character has agency or whether a story has escaped the able gaze. A programme could contain several disabled characters and still portray them badly. Conversely, one complicated, funny and contradictory disabled character might achieve more than a larger number of thinly written roles.

Sky presents a different challenge. Its [inclusive-production targets](https://www.skygroup.sky/about/work-with-us/production/inclusive-production?utm_source=chatgpt.com) say that 10% of those appearing on screen should appear disabled to audiences. Diamond's Perceived data appears designed to measure precisely that.

But the apparent fit conceals unresolved questions. Is the 10% based on people, characters, roles, episodes or contributions? Who decides whether someone "appears" disabled? Are smaller roles included? Can repeated appearances by one character inflate the result? Has the perception measure been independently tested?

Diamond may be able to produce a number, but producing a number is not the same as proving that it measures the intended outcome.

UKTV's 15% Deaf, disabled or neurodivergent target is more straightforwardly about representation. Diamond Actual data can make a meaningful contribution to monitoring it. Yet that target should not be reinterpreted as evidence that 15% of characters appear disabled.

ITV and Paramount add further variations involving commissioning investment, production leadership, stories, leading roles and access. Each commitment needs its own evidence. A single cross-industry disability figure cannot simultaneously measure workforce participation, accessible production, story perspective, apparent disability and authentic casting.

### Access creates opportunity, but does not prove casting

## CDN deserves substantial credit for its work beyond Diamond

Doubling Disability focused attention on employment and helped increase disabled participation behind the camera. The [TV Access Project's 5As](https://www.creativediversitynetwork.com/tv-access-project/the-5as) — Anticipate, Ask, Assess, Adjust and Advocate — offer practical guidance for making production accessible. These initiatives address conditions that have kept disabled people out of television.

But access is an enabling condition, not an on-screen result.

A production can appoint an access coordinator, provide adjustments and meet workforce targets without casting a disabled performer. Another production might employ disabled crew while presenting no apparent disability to audiences. These remain valuable achievements. They simply should not be used as substitutes for portrayal evidence.

The same care is needed when interpreting CDN's governance. Its board is composed of representatives nominated by its member organisations. This gives CDN influence, shared infrastructure and access to broadcasters. It also means the organisations being measured participate in governing the body doing the measuring.

That does not imply that the data is manipulated. It does strengthen the case for independent review, published coding rules and disabled-led oversight.

Member involvement gives the system operational legitimacy. Independent and disabled-led scrutiny would give it greater evidential legitimacy.

## The missing link is authentic casting

If broadcasters want to know whether their creative promises have changed what audiences see, casting choices must become part of the analysis.

That does not mean asking researchers to diagnose performers from photographs or performances. Disability identity should be based on consented information or reliable public self-identification. Uncertainty should be recorded rather than converted into "not disabled."

The study would need to link four things:

- the performer's documented disability identity;
- the character and prominence of the role;
- how disability becomes perceptible within the performance or its context;
- whether the role was explicitly disabled or disability became present through casting.

A representative sample should be reviewed by more than one assessor, with disabled people involved in designing and applying the framework. Disagreements should be reported, not hidden. Results should distinguish people from roles, roles from episodes and characters from contributions.

This would allow the industry to ask more revealing questions.

- Are disabled performers concentrated in stories about disability, or are they becoming part of television's everyday population?
- Do disabled actors get to play authority, romance, ambition, danger and moral complexity, or mainly vulnerability and care?
- When a production says it practises incidental casting, where are those performers in its fictional world?
- Do supporting casts reflect society more convincingly than lead casts, or does disability disappear as roles become smaller?
- And perhaps most importantly: are broadcasters using disability only when a script demands it, or recognising disabled actors as a source of creative possibility?

Diamond has helped British television see who is working within the industry. That is a real achievement.

The next step is harder. The industry must learn to see what its casting choices communicate and who remains absent when the script does not explicitly ask for disability.
