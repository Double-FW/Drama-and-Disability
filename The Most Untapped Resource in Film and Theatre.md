# The Most Untapped Resource in Film and Theatre? Disabled Actors

For too long, Deaf and disabled actors have struggled to access not only their own stories but also the opportunity to be cast incidentally, in roles not defined by disability. While a few exceptional figures like [Esmond Knight](http://www.esmondknight.org.uk/) in the UK and [Lionel Barrymore](https://www.imdb.com/name/nm0000859/) in the US carved out careers during the 20th century, the doors of drama schools and major studios remained largely closed. Only in the past few decades, thanks to [disability-led theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_and_disability) and a shift in attitudes at drama schools and commissioning bodies, has a new generation of disabled talent begun to emerge.

Yet despite this progress, resistance remains. The industry continues to recycle four familiar excuses for excluding disabled actors. Each of them demands an answer.

### 1\. "There is no talent."

Historically, [disabled actors were exceptions rather than the rule](https://www.imdb.com/list/ls4150522203/), often already well-connected. Access to classical training was systematically blocked, limiting career pathways. But that has changed. Since the 1990s, disability-led theatre has become a training ground for performers, and in the last decade hundreds of disabled actors have graduated from drama schools. Platforms like [**ProFile Performers**](https://profileperformers.com/) or [**IMDB**](https://www.imdb.com/list/ls541464229/) showcase this talent to casting directors worldwide.

There is also an assumption that to cast a disabled actor they have to have an identical disability to the character. There are some practical exceptions such as if the character is a native sign language speaker, or if they use a wheelchair, are a certain hight or need to have a particular number of limbs, but in most cases the story isn't the condition, it is the experience of being a disabled person with a lived experience of otherness. This is why [Richard III has and can be transposed](https://medium.com/@AccessibilityandInclusion/recasting-richard-iii-age-disability-and-the-future-of-shakespeare-a1719378fa7d) to work with actors of different disabilities. For Richard his scoliosis isn't the story, the ableism he experienced is.

The claim that "there is no talent" is no longer credible.

### 2\. "There are no scripts."

There are two answers to this misconception:

- **Authentic portrayal**: Disabled characters exist in abundance. From literary and historical figures to popular universes like [Marvel, DC](https://medium.com/@garethfordwilliams/disability-in-marvel-and-dc-part-1-disabled-characters-are-interesting-7ab3c2cac1eb), Star Wars, [Star Trek](https://zinebakery.com/assets/redistro/dark-zines/StarTrekAndDisability_V1-DARK.pdf), and Tolkien, there is no shortage of disabled characters. In fact, [23 Academy Award nominations have gone to actors playing visibly disabled characters](https://www.imdb.com/list/ls597302276). The problem? [Around 80% of all disabled roles still go to non-disabled actors](https://www.imdb.com/list/ls591004208), a practice known as [cripping-up](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cripping-up), or disability is erased entirely in adaptations to simplify casting.
- **Incidental portrayal**: Scripts rarely dictate how a character enters a room. Why assume they walked? Characters can be parents, lovers, teachers, criminals, or leaders regardless of disability. Esmond Knight, a blind actor, built a career playing sighted characters. Zak Ford-Williams, who has quadriplegic cerebral palsy, has [already challenged the myth that progressive conditions can't be portrayed authentically](https://medium.com/@AccessibilityandInclusion/why-casting-disabled-actors-in-roles-with-progressive-disabilities-isnt-just-possible-it-s-c6593d950394).

The barrier here isn't the script, it's imagination.

### 3\. "It's too expensive."

This is perhaps the weakest excuse. Some disabled actors may require adjustments, but others need none at all. Access riders outline requirements in advance, and organisations like [**TAP**](https://www.bbc.co.uk/creativediversity/i-hub/home/tv-access-project) (The TV Access Project) and [disability consultants](https://www.casarotto.co.uk/access-team)provide clear guidance.

If someone claims casting a disabled actor is "too expensive," ask to see their costings. They won't have any. These assumptions are excuses rooted in bias, not reality. When productions plan collaboratively with disabled performers, the creative output is indistinguishable in quality, but often richer in perspective.

### 4\. "Anyone should be able to play anything."

In principle, yes — but only if the playing field is level. Currently, disabled actors are excluded not just from non-disabled roles but from most disabled roles too. Around 80% of disabled characters on screen are played by non-disabled actors. Until that imbalance is corrected, the idea of "best actor for the job" is not fairness , it's a cover for exclusion.

Worse still, film adaptations frequently downplay or erase disability altogether. This pattern is particularly common in franchises like Marvel and DC, erasing opportunities that should have existed in the first place.

True equity would mean disabled actors being considered for every role, disabled and non-disabled alike. But that can only happen after systemic bias is dismantled and authentic casting becomes standard practice.

### From Box-Ticking to Creative Opportunity

The industry will not change unless it reframes disability. It is not a diversity quota to fill, nor a burden to accommodate. Disability is a creative opportunity.

As actor Zak Ford-Williams puts it:

"Creativity is about making interesting choices, and disability can be an interesting choice."

Directors, casting agents, and producers pride themselves on taking bold risks. Incidental disability casting isn't a risk at all, it's an untapped resource that can make stories richer, characters deeper, and audiences more engaged.

The future of disability portrayal depends on one simple shift: from viewing disability as a problem to solve, to seeing it as one of the most powerful creative opportunities in storytelling today.
