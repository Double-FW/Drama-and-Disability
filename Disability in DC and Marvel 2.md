# Disability in DC and Marvel: Creating Disability Storylines in Cinematic Universes

_"Creativity is about making interesting choices, and disability can be an interesting choice."_

Marvel and DC have spent more than 80 years imagining what happens when bodies change.

Characters are transformed by radiation, magic, alien biology, military experiments, inherited powers and technologies from the future. They lose powers and regain them. They exchange bodies, merge with machines, cross into alternate realities and survive injuries that would ordinarily end a life. Some are born visibly different whilst others' bodies change over time.  
Some characters depend on armour, prosthetics, artificial organs or complex technology simply to remain alive. Most experience "otherness", prejudice and bigotry because of their bodily, communication or neurological differences.

These stories are already full of disability ideas.

Without changing a line of text there are countless creative opportunities to look at those ideas more closely and ask what they might reveal about the characters, the worlds they inhabit and the audiences watching them.

What happens when a superhero's body does not behave in the way other people expect? What does a superpower feel like when it is also a form of access? What is the difference between technology that enables someone and technology that controls them? How does someone build an identity when other people see their body before they see the person?

These are not questions designed to satisfy an inclusion target. They are creative questions. They generate character, visual language, relationships, action, humour, jeopardy and subtext. They can influence writing, casting, costume, performance, production design, sound and publicity.

The characters and themes already exist. The possibility lies in connecting them more deliberately with disability identity and lived experience, then seeing where that creative conversation leads.

# Otherness has always been part of the story

The X-Men provide the clearest starting point because their stories are built around what it means to be identified as different by the society around you. Since their creation in 1963, mutants have been used to explore prejudice, belonging, identity and the pressure to conform. The metaphor has supported readings connected with racism, antisemitism, sexism, homophobia, transphobia, ableism and many other forms of social exclusion.

Disability belongs naturally within that landscape because so many X-Men are othered through their bodies. Some mutants can choose whether to reveal their difference. Others cannot. Nightcrawler cannot enter a room without people seeing blue skin, a tail and a body that immediately marks him as unfamiliar. Chamber's mutation has transformed his face and chest. Toad's physicality changes the way people respond to him before they know anything about his character. Beast may be a scientist, diplomat and intellectual, yet strangers can still decide what he is before he speaks.

Cyclops depends on technology to interact safely with the world. Rogue must manage physical contact and self-isolation. Professor Xavier uses a wheelchair. Forge has a prosthetic leg and cybernetic hand. Karma becomes an amputee. Blindfold and Destiny are blind.

These characters do not share one experience, and they should not be reduced to a single disability metaphor. What connects them is the tension between who they understand themselves to be and what other people believe their bodies mean. That tension is a rich place for a writer to begin and these themes have become foundational for the comic universes.

How does Nightcrawler recognise the difference between curiosity, fear and hostility? Does Beast change the way he speaks because he knows that people have already made assumptions about his intelligence? Does Cyclops experience his visor as a weapon, a safety system, an access tool or all three? What does Rogue notice about the casual physical contact between other people? How does Forge feel when someone admires the technology of his prosthetic body without acknowledging the person using it?

None of these questions requires a simple answer. Their purpose is to create detail.

This is where disability lived experience can enrich an existing theme without turning a story into a lesson. The X-Men already explore visibility, masking, disclosure and belonging. Disabled performers and creatives may recognise dimensions within those ideas that have not yet been fully explored on screen.

A character who has spent a lifetime being stared at may know exactly how to make an entrance. Someone who is routinely underestimated may understand the strategic value of allowing that assumption to continue. A person whose body attracts attention may use clothing, humour, stillness or confidence to control how other people look at them.

These are choices that affect performance and direction. They can be expressed through framing, eye lines, pauses and physical positioning. The camera might initially reproduce the stare, allowing the audience to experience the character as a collection of differences, before changing its relationship with them and drawing our attention towards humour, authority, intelligence or vulnerability.

The character has not changed. The way we see them has. That is a cinematic idea.

# Disability does not need to arrive as a lesson

A future disability storyline does not have to begin with a diagnosis, an accident or a scene explaining what happened. It can begin with a character noticing something.

Imagine a superhero team preparing to enter an unfamiliar building. The conventional purpose of the scene may be to distribute information before the action starts. A disability-informed perspective creates additional possibilities. One character may study the entrances rather than the building's exterior. Another may notice that an alarm will make spoken communication impossible. Someone else may question whether the route described as accessible will still be usable once the power fails.

These details do not need to stop the story. They can reveal competence. The person asking the questions may be the most experienced member of the team. Their knowledge may be practical and tactical. It may save everyone.

Disability can enter a story through preparation, improvisation, impatience, confidence or humour. The audience does not always need an explanation. Sometimes it is enough to recognise that a character knows something the others do not.

This is particularly valuable in superhero stories because so much of heroism is expressed through preparedness. Batman is admired because he anticipates problems. Iron Man continually redesigns his technology. The X-Men train to use different abilities together. Disability lived experience can bring another form of strategic knowledge into those worlds.

A wheelchair user may think about routes, gradients and surfaces before anyone else. A Deaf character may plan communication before entering a visually restricted environment. Someone with chronic pain may understand pacing, energy and recovery. A blind character may notice a sound pattern that sighted teammates have filtered out.

The point is not that disability gives people magical compensating powers. The opportunity lies in experience, attention and adaptation.

## The body can remain an open creative question

Superhero films often treat the body as an outcome. The hero receives an idealised body. The villain receives a threatening one. A transformation shows the audience what the character has become.

Disability offers a more interesting possibility: the body can remain a question. What does this particular body allow the character to understand? How has it shaped their habits? What do they protect, reveal or disguise? How do they respond when someone offers help? What changes when help is assumed rather than requested?

A character's disability may be explicit in one scene and incidental in the next. An amputee superhero may discuss the design of a prosthetic before a mission, then spend the following scene arguing about politics, flirting with someone or making a poor decision that has nothing to do with disability. That range is important because disability identity can shape a character without containing them.

The most interesting portrayals often emerge when a performer is allowed to decide when disability is foregrounded and when it simply exists. A movement, glance or adjustment may communicate something that the script does not need to explain. This is where casting becomes part of the writing process rather than the final stage of it. A disabled performer does not simply arrive to reproduce a finished character. Their body, instincts and experience can alter how the character is conceived.

They may recognise that the character would position themselves differently in a room. They may suggest that a supposedly supportive line would feel unnecessary or patronising. They may see humour in a situation the writer had imagined as solemn. They may know that a problem presented as new would have been solved years earlier.

These are not corrections at the edge of the creative process. They are new possibilities at its centre.

## Mutation as amplified lived experience

Mutant stories often take recognisable experiences and enlarge them.

A person may feel unable to hide the way they look, move or communicate.  
A mutant takes that visibility to a fantastical scale.

Someone may feel pressure to mask an aspect of themselves.  
A mutant may literally transform their appearance in order to pass.

Someone may fear that disclosure will change how colleagues, friends or partners treat them.  
A mutant may know that revealing their identity could end a relationship, a career or their safety.

Someone may depend on medication, equipment or routines.  
A mutant may require technology to contain a power that could otherwise place themselves or other people at risk.

This amplification creates room to connect fantasy with lived experience without assigning fictional characters real-world diagnoses.

A writer exploring Rogue might speak with people who manage touch, immune vulnerability, chronic pain or sensory boundaries. A story about Cyclops could be informed by blind and visually impaired people, people who depend on medical devices, or anyone whose safety relies on equipment that cannot be allowed to fail. Chamber could invite conversations with people who have facial difference, speech difference or experience of communicating through technology.

The purpose would not be to decide that Rogue "represents" one condition or that Cyclops should be understood through a single diagnostic lens. It would be to discover practical and emotional truths that make the fantasy more convincing.

How does a person explain a physical boundary without making every social encounter about that boundary? What happens when someone else touches equipment that is part of your access? How does it feel to know that one system failure could change everything? How much trust is required before someone else can help maintain the technology on which you rely?

These questions belong naturally within superhero storytelling because they are questions about power, responsibility and control.

## When technology becomes part of the person and their identity

Marvel and DC are full of characters whose bodies incorporate machines.

Iron Man depends on technology to remain alive and operate as a superhero. Cyborg's body is rebuilt through systems designed by his father. The Winter Soldier's arm is both prosthesis and weapon. Nebula's body records years of modification imposed by Thanos. Cable lives with a techno-organic condition that continually changes him. Deathlok characters are reconstructed by organisations that often retain power over their new bodies. Misty Knight uses a bionic arm. War Machine uses armour and an exoskeletal mobility system.

These characters invite questions that extend far beyond whether the technology looks impressive.

- Who controls the update?
- Who owns the data produced by the body?
- Can the equipment be removed?
- Does the user have a choice?
- What happens when it fails?
- Can someone else disable it?
- Is it comfortable?
- Does it work differently when the person is tired, frightened or injured?
- Was it designed around the user, or is the user expected to adapt to the machine?

These are familiar questions for many people who use assistive and medical technology. A wheelchair, hearing aid, prosthetic limb or communication device may provide access, but it can also depend on batteries, repairs, software, specialist services and environments that support it. The technology may be liberating and frustrating at the same time. It may be part of the person's identity while also being something they would like to replace or improve.

A superhero version of that experience can be spectacular without losing its humanity.

Imagine Cyborg recognising that an apparently harmless system is attempting to gain access to his body. Imagine the Winter Soldier choosing a prosthetic designed around his own priorities rather than accepting one created for combat. Imagine War Machine explaining that being able to stand in an exoskeleton does not mean every environment has become accessible. Imagine Nebula modifying one of the systems imposed on her until it finally reflects her own choices.

The technology is not merely a prop. It is a relationship.

Cyborg stories are often described as being about the boundary between human and machine, but the more interesting question to explore may be why those categories are assumed to be separate?

For Victor Stone, his technological body may be part of him even when it originated in decisions made without his consent. For Nebula, mechanical components can represent abuse, survival, strength and unwanted memory at the same time. For the Winter Soldier, the prosthetic arm may carry the history of control while also becoming something he chooses to use.

The creative questions become increasingly personal.

When did the technology begin to feel like part of the character?  
Which elements do they consider their own?  
Which still feel imposed?  
Can a body contain memories?  
Does removing a device feel like freedom, vulnerability or loss?  
What happens when other people are more interested in the technology than the person using it?

These questions can shape action and intimacy equally. A battle may be about bodily control. A romantic scene may involve trust around augmentation. A family argument may centre on who was allowed to make a medical decision. A quiet scene may show a character maintaining their own body after everyone else has gone.

The spectacle becomes more meaningful because it is connected to a person's identity.

## From assistive technology to superpower

Comic-book universes frequently take an access need and amplify it into something extraordinary.

A prosthetic becomes stronger than a biological arm.  
A mobility device becomes a weapons platform.  
A visor contains energy capable of cutting through buildings.  
Armour enables flight.  
A hearing system detects signals beyond ordinary human perception.

This could be thought of as overcompensating assistive technology: equipment that does not simply reproduce an expected function but creates an entirely new one.

That idea has enormous creative potential.

The most interesting question is not whether the technology has made the person better than they were before. It is how the technology changes the choices available to them.

A conventional prosthetic may be designed to resemble a hand.  
A superhero prosthetic could become any tool its user imagines.  
A wheelchair may be designed for streets, buildings or flight.  
A fictional mobility system might travel through space, climb vertical surfaces or create its own accessible environment.  
Crutches might also be web-shooters.  
A cane might hold energy, information or a weapon.

The assistive device remains recognisable, but the genre allows it to become something more.

Writers and designers could begin by working with disabled actors or writers who already customise, adapt and reinterpret their equipment. What would they invent if the usual limitations of materials, budgets and physics no longer applied?  
What would they keep?  
What would they discard?  
Would the fictional device imitate a conventional body, or would it develop an entirely new visual language?

These conversations could create designs or uses of existing designs that have never appeared on screen because they begin with people who already think creatively about their own access. This is not representation added after the main idea. It is research and development for imagination.

## Acquiring a disability as the beginning of a story

Several established comic-book characters acquire disabilities during their stories. Aquaman loses a hand, U.S. Agent loses an arm and a leg, Roy Harper uses a cybernetic arm, Karma loses a leg, Bishop acquires a prosthetic arm, and Hellion loses both hands. In the Age of Apocalypse timeline, even Wolverine — whose body usually repairs itself — must live with a permanent loss.

These moments do not have to be temporary setbacks before the character is restored. They can begin a new chapter in which someone already certain of who they are must reconsider their identity, relationships and understanding of strength.

The most interesting questions are often emotional.

Does the character still recognise themselves?  
Do teammates become protective, distant or uncomfortable?  
Does support feel welcome, intrusive or both?  
How do they respond when other people see the disability before they see the hero?..

Aquaman offers a particularly rich example. His different replacements, a hook, a magical water hand or advanced Atlantean technology, could each reflect a different stage in his acceptance of change. The real question is not whether losing a hand makes him stronger or weaker, but what it means for a king whose identity has always been tied to physical power, heritage and control.

The same is true of John Walker, whose confidence rests on military capability and his claim to a heroic legacy. Acquiring a disability could force him to ask whether strength comes from the body, the uniform or the choices he makes.

For Wolverine, permanent limb loss would challenge the certainty that his body will always return to what it was. A character defined by survival would have to discover the difference between healing and accepting change.

These are not simply injury stories. They are stories about identity, vulnerability and the emotional work of becoming someone new without ceasing to be yourself.

These events do not need to function as endings or temporary diversions before the character is restored. They can begin a new creative chapter as the change is not just their but for everyone around them. These are not simply injury stories, they are identity stories and they stimulate interesting questions for the storytellers:

How does an established hero respond when familiar movement no longer produces familiar results?  
What knowledge do they suddenly need that they did not value before?  
How do teammates behave differently around them?  
Who becomes unexpectedly helpful or empathetic?  
Who becomes uncomfortable or shows their biases?  
Does the character want their old fighting style back, or do they begin creating something more appropriate to who they are now?

## Disability without an origin story or identity

One of the most promising creative directions is to cast disabled actors in roles where disability is not specified at all.

A scientist can use a wheelchair.  
An Asgardian can have a limb difference.  
A S.H.I.E.L.D. agent can be Deaf.  
A journalist can use a communication device.  
A sorcerer can have a tremor.  
A Green Lantern can have a prosthetic leg.

None of these choices requires an origin story. The character had a life before the film began, and disability can simply be part of that life: visible to the audience, familiar to the people around them and never reduced to the purpose of the plot.

Its presence would still change the scene.

It may alter how another character offers help, shows respect, expresses attraction or makes assumptions about authority and competence. It may reveal who is comfortable with difference, who becomes awkward and who has absorbed prejudices they do not yet recognise in themselves. Relationships gain subtext because the audience can see how culture and expectation operate around the disabled character, even when nobody names them.

This is where incidental portrayal becomes especially interesting. It suggests that disability exists throughout the universe rather than appearing only when a story wants to discuss it. The social model then becomes a creative tool: instead of asking what is wrong with the character, writers can examine the environments, technologies, customs and power structures surrounding them. A disabled character may move confidently through an advanced society designed for many kinds of bodies, then encounter barriers within a culture that considers itself more enlightened. An alien civilisation may understand access and equality completely differently from Earth.

The visible presence of unwritten disability changes the relationship with each member of the audience, challenging their expectations of who they expected in the role. The actor's body introduces meaning that is not contained in the dialogue, changing how we read status, beauty, power, vulnerability and identity. A hero, lover, leader or villain no longer looks exactly as convention has taught us to expect, and that difference invites the audience to notice the expectations they brought with them.

The character does not need to explain their disability. Their presence is already doing creative work.

## Teams, relationships and interdependence

Superhero teams are usually assembled around complementary powers, but the most interesting dynamics often emerge between the battles. Disability can change the emotional life of a group: who trusts whom, who listens, who assumes, who protects, who patronises and who quietly learns how another person needs to be supported.

A disabled team member may notice things others miss, not because disability grants a compensating superpower, but because experience shapes attention. A blind character may be more alert to changes in someones voice or intonation, changes in the way a floor sounds or a surface feels. A Deaf character may notice visual information others overlook such as body language or because others were distracted by a sound the moment a mask slips. Someone with chronic pain may understand limits, pacing and recovery in ways the rest of the team has never had to consider. That knowledge and detail can become valuable, but it in itself is not a "superpower" and should not be the only reason the character belongs.

What matters even more is how the team responds.

One person may learn when to offer help and when to wait. Another may become overprotective, confusing care with control. Someone may avoid talking about disability altogether because they are frightened of saying the wrong thing, while another may make assumptions about what the disabled character can contribute. These small reactions can expose the politics and unconscious biases within a group more effectively than a speech ever could, and create a new barrier they have to overcome.

Disability can also create intimacy. A teammate may know how to repair a prosthetic, charge a communication device or recognise when pain is being hidden. These acts can become part of friendship, trust or love, not because the disabled character is dependent, but because all relationships involve forms of interdependence.

The quieter moments are where this becomes most human. A piece of equipment may be maintained during an argument. A character may ask before touching someone's chair. A lover may understand the difference between assistance and intrusion. A team may gradually develop its own language, routines and ways of supporting one another.

Care can be generous, awkward, political and deeply personal. In a superhero team, it can also be part of what makes them both flawed and heroic.

## Disabled villains and difficult creative choices

Disabled characters should be allowed the same moral range as everyone else. They can be generous, selfish, brave, manipulative, loving, cruel or dangerous. Human flaws are interesting, and disability should not prevent a character from becoming a compelling villain any more than it should require them to become a hero.

Many disabled villains have also become iconic. Removing their disability does not improve the character; it often removes part of the history, physical presence or social experience that made them distinctive. The more interesting creative challenge is to retain the disability while making it clear that impairment itself is not the source of their cruelty.

Sometimes disability can remain entirely incidental. A villain may use a wheelchair, have a limb difference or live with sight loss without the story needing to explain how this relates to their motives. They are disabled, and they are also ambitious, jealous, violent or obsessed with control. One identity does not have to account for the other.

Other characters invite a more complicated interpretation. As with Shakespeare's Richard III, prejudice and rejection can become part of the emotional history that intensifies qualities already present. Ableism may have hardened resentment, encouraged isolation or taught the character to use fear before others can use it against them. Ableism does not create psychopathy, but it may help explain how an existing capacity for anger or control became supercharged by their experience.

That distinction matters. A disabled villain can be both dangerous and a victim of prejudice. They may understand exclusion or abuse with complete clarity while choosing to inflict it on others. They may recognise how institutions misuse power because they have experienced it themselves, then build an equally controlling system of their own. Those contradictions make a character richer, not more sympathetic by default.

Casting therefore needs balance. An authentically disabled performer should not be asked to soften the villain or turn them into positive representation, but their lived experience may reveal where confidence ends and defensiveness begins, where intimidation is deliberate and where it has been learned. The disability remains real, the villainy remains chosen, and the space between them becomes one of the most interesting parts of the character.

## Lived experience as a source of creative questions

The most useful creative consultation rarely begins with the question, "Is this accurate?"

A better starting point may be, "What have we not considered?"

A disabled consultant, actor or writer may notice that a scene assumes equipment works without maintenance. They may recognise that a line intended to sound supportive would feel patronising. They may suggest that the character would position themselves differently in the room. They may identify humour that comes from familiarity rather than ridicule.

They may also propose an entirely different approach to technology or a detail about inter character relationships that had not occurred to anyone else.

This is where lived experience becomes creatively generative.

The consultant is not there simply to remove mistakes. They can introduce possibilities.

The actor is not there simply to embody a decision that has already been made. Their presence can alter the decision.

A production might begin with the theme rather than the impairment. Is the story about belonging, control, transformation, legacy, dependence, power, visibility, family or technology? The team can then ask where disability lived experience might deepen that theme.

· If the story is about control, which characters have experienced other people making decisions about their bodies?  
· If it is about belonging, who can conceal their difference and who cannot?· If it is about technology, who depends on the system and who owns it?  
· If it is about legacy, how might an acquired disability change the way a hero understands the person they were expected to become?  
· If it is about otherness, which characters are treated as unfamiliar or threatening because of how they look, move or communicate?  
· If it is about prejudice, how have other people's assumptions shaped the character's relationships, ambitions and sense of self?  
· If it is about visibility, who has control over when their difference is seen, and who is denied that choice?  
· If it is about power, does the character use the prejudice they have experienced to challenge a system, survive within it or reproduce it against other?

From there, the team can identify people with relevant lived experience. Not to provide a single approved answer, but to widen the number of questions available. The story can then evolve through writing, casting, design and performance.

## The story beyond the screen

Authentic casting does not end when the cameras stop. A disabled performer brings a story into the production that audiences can continue to engage with long after the film or series has finished.

Disability storylines have produced some of cinema's most celebrated performances because audiences are drawn to difference, transformation and lives that do not follow familiar patterns. Yet the industry still tends to regard disability as a subject to portray rather than a source of talent, creative knowledge and public interest. Casting a disabled actor changes that relationship. The story is no longer only about a character's experience; it is also informed by someone who understands aspects of that experience from within.

This creates rich material for publicity, but it does not need to be framed through triumph or inspiration. The more interesting story is about craft. How did the actor's lived experience change the writing? What did they bring to the character that was not originally on the page? How did their body influence costume, choreography, production design or the way a relationship was performed? What did the director understand differently after working with them?

Disabled actors often arrive with personal histories that are unusually relevant to the worlds being created around them. They may have had to develop persistence, adaptability and a detailed awareness of how environments, institutions and other people respond to difference. Those experiences can shape a performance in ways that cannot be recreated through research alone, while also giving journalists and audiences a more substantial story about how the production was made.

Otherness is interesting because it creates contrast, tension and curiosity. When a disabled performer enters a familiar cinematic universe, the casting can make an established character feel newly visible and give audiences a reason to look again. It can also introduce a performer whose own journey adds meaning to the production without becoming more important than their talent.

The strongest publicity does not congratulate the studio for including someone. It shows what became possible because that actor was there. The performer is not a symbol added to the campaign after the creative work is complete; they are one of the people who made the work more distinctive, truthful and worth talking about.

# Disability in DC and Marvel: Could the Answer to Disability Portrayal be found in the Multiverse?

## n a universe of infinite possibility must surely find infinite opportunity for inclusive creativity?

The multiverse has changed the rules of superhero storytelling.

It allows several versions of the same character to exist at once. One may be older, younger, heroic, corrupt, human, alien, animated or living in a world where history took an entirely different path. Characters can change gender, ethnicity, nationality, ability, personality, costume, powers and moral purpose while remaining recognisably themselves.

Spider-Man can be Peter Parker, Miles Morales, Gwen Stacy, a private detective, a cartoon pig or a dinosaur. Loki can meet versions of himself who differ in age, gender, species and temperament. Batman can be a Victorian detective, a vampire, a revolutionary, a tyrant or the survivor of a world in which Bruce Wayne never became Batman at all.

This is not a flaw in the concept. It is the concept.

A multiverse asks us to accept that every decision creates another possibility. Somewhere, events happened differently. Someone survived an accident, lost a battle, made another choice or inherited a different body. The character remains familiar, but their relationship with the world changes. If all of that is possible, then every hero, villain, friend, lover, victim, colleague and boss must also have multiple disabled selves. Not one symbolic disabled universe, set apart from the others and visited for a special episode, even though that would theoretically exist, but countless disabled versions of every character.

There are blind versions of Batman somewhere in the multiverse. There are Deaf Lois Lanes, amputee Thors, wheelchair-using Peter Parkers and Wonder Women with cerebral palsy. Somewhere, Lex Luthor uses a screen reader, Asgardian warriors have limb differences, and many Amazons live with chronic pain. Somewhere, disability has always been part of every character, and nobody considers it remarkable enough to explain.

That possibility is not an intrusion into the multiverse. It follows directly from its logic.

Once a fictional universe accepts infinite variation, the repeated absence of disabled people begins to look less like coincidence and more like a creative boundary imposed by the people deciding who gets to exist.

## What remains when the body changes?

Disability is part of human variation. It can be present from birth, emerge gradually or arrive suddenly through illness, injury, conflict or age. In a multiverse built around infinite combinations of history, chance and biology, disabled versions of familiar characters are not merely possible. They are inevitable and innumerable.

Some versions of a character may have a condition closely connected with their powers. Others may be disabled for reasons that have nothing to do with being a superhero. Bruce Wayne may lose his sight during his training and build a different Batman around sound, touch, memory and technology. Diana Prince may have a limb difference that is completely ordinary on Themyscira. Clark Kent might be Deaf, with Kryptonian technology and Smallville sign language forming part of his childhood rather than being introduced as a later complication.

The point is not to find a disability that symbolically matches each hero. It is to ask what versions of the character become possible when disability is part of their life.

What does Batman notice when he does not receive information in the same way as earlier screen versions?  
How might Spider-Man move if web-swinging works alongside a mobility device rather than replacing it?  
How would a Deaf Superman experience the world when his other senses are superhuman?  
What would an Amazonian prosthetic look like after thousands of years of independent technological development?  
How might a Green Lantern ring respond to a wearer whose body or communication differs from the assumptions built into the Corps?

These are not questions about reducing a character's abilities. They are questions about character, world-building and performance. They encourage writers and audiences to reconsider what is genuinely essential. Is Spider-Man defined by a particular body, or by responsibility, improvisation and the decision to help when someone is in danger? Is Batman defined by physical symmetry, or by preparation, intelligence, obsession and his refusal to stop? Is Thor recognisable because of the shape of the actor playing him, or because of his power, pride, history and complicated relationship with worthiness?

Disability does not have to take anything away from these identities. It can reveal which parts of them mattered all along.

## The Spider-Verse has already proved the principle

The Spider-Verse may be the clearest demonstration of how far a character can change while retaining a shared emotional foundation. Its different Spider-people vary enormously in age, culture, species, visual design and history. Yet responsibility, loss, courage and the decision to keep going continue to connect them.

Sun-Spider, also known as Charlotte Webber, provides an especially valuable example. Charlotte has Ehlers-Danlos syndrome and uses a wheelchair and crutches. Her crutches are fitted with web-shooters. She does not abandon her mobility equipment before she is allowed to become part of the Spider-Verse. The equipment becomes part of her superhero design.

That is a small but important creative shift. It does not ask how Charlotte can be made to move like every previous Spider-Man. It asks what Spider-person movement looks like when it begins with Charlotte. A conventional approach might regard crutches as something that complicates the established silhouette. A more imaginative approach recognises that the complication is the idea. They change her reach, rhythm, balance and relationship with the webbing. They create different poses, different methods of movement and different action possibilities.

The result is not a disabled imitation of Spider-Man. It is another Spider-person and the writers have already shown us that this works.

Once a multiverse contains a character such as Sun-Spider, it becomes difficult to argue that iconic roles must always be cast according to one narrow physical template. The mythology has already accepted that Spider-Man can survive radical change. It has accepted that anyone can wear the mask. The next step is believing that during casting and the only thing holding that back are limitations in the imaginations of the decision makers.

## The multiverse removes the usual excuses

Objections to disability casting are often framed as concerns about fidelity. The character has always looked a certain way. The audience expects a familiar physical presence. Disability was not included in the original story. Changing the character would supposedly make the adaptation less authentic.

The multiverse makes those arguments increasingly difficult to sustain.

These franchises alter characters constantly. Origins are rewritten. Relationships are removed, combined or invented. Costumes change. Powers are expanded, reduced or transferred. Characters who are dead in one continuity are alive in another. Heroes become villains. Villains become heroes. Human beings become aliens, robots, gods, animals and zombies.

Disability is no more disruptive to continuity than any of these choices. In many cases, it is considerably less disruptive.

Casting a disabled actor as a scientist, lawyer, journalist, military officer or sorcerer may require no change to the dialogue at all because the writers may not have explicitly stated that the character must not be disabled, or that they can only enter a room by walking. A wheelchair, prosthetic limb, hearing aid, facial difference or distinctive way of moving can simply be part of the character's physical presence.

The actor does not need to arrive from a universe labelled as the disabled one. They can come from a world in which this is simply what the character looks like.

Nor does a disabled interpretation have to replace every existing version. The multiverse allows different forms of a character to coexist. A disabled Peter Parker does not erase Tom Holland, Andrew Garfield or Tobey Maguire. Another Batman does not invalidate Michael Keaton, Christian Bale or Robert Pattinson.

Recasting has always been part of superhero storytelling because audiences are ultimately attached to characters who are larger than any one performance.

**The multiverse is a creative laboratory.** That creates a remarkably simple opportunity, so the next or a parallel version can be different.

This is particularly important for incidental casting. Disabled actors are still too often considered only after a script explicitly announces a disabled role. If disability is not written into the dialogue, casting can default to the assumption that the character must be non-disabled.

A multiverse exposes the narrowness of that assumption.

Unless the story depends upon one precise physical characteristic, disability is already possible. The script may not need to change at all. What changes is the creative team's understanding of who can inhabit the role. A visibly disabled Lex Luthor might understand exactly what it feels like to be judged by appearance yet still build systems that classify and control others. A Deaf Lois Lane might be especially alert to who is included in a conversation and who controls information. A wheelchair-using Bruce Wayne might experience architecture, wealth and public space differently from previous versions while remaining privileged, obsessive and extraordinarily prepared.

These choices do not make the characters morally better. They make them more specific.

## Acquired disability does not have to end the character

The multiverse is not the only way to introduce disabled versions of familiar characters. Disability already happens within many comic-book storylines.

Aquaman has lost a hand in several continuities. Roy Harper has used a cybernetic arm. U.S. Agent has lost an arm and a leg. Karma becomes an amputee. Bishop acquires a cybernetic arm. Hellion loses both hands and controls prosthetic replacements through telekinesis. In the Age of Apocalypse timeline, Wolverine permanently loses a hand.

Screen stories have introduced acquired disability too. Felicity Smoak becomes paralysed after being shot in Arrow. James Rhodes sustains a spinal injury in the Marvel Cinematic Universe and begins using an exoskeletal mobility system. Phil Coulson loses a hand and receives several cybernetic replacements.

These stories raise a question that superhero franchises have rarely been willing to consider fully: when a character becomes disabled, why must the same non-disabled performer continue simulating that disability indefinitely? And at one point does that decision become uncomfortable to the audience and even creatively inappropriate?

Recasting is not always the only answer. An acquired-disability storyline can be developed thoughtfully with an established performer, disabled writers, consultants, body doubles, stunt professionals, CGI and movement specialists. But recasting should not be treated as unimaginable.

Superhero roles are recast routinely.

Audiences have accepted numerous actors as Batman, Spider-Man, Superman, the Joker, Catwoman, Lex Luthor, the Penguin and Professor Xavier. A new performer may arrive because the timeline has changed, because the production wants a different age or tone, or because the franchise has simply started again.

Acquired disability could be an equally legitimate creative reason.

A story might move forward several years and introduce a disabled performer as the next stage of the character's life. A multiverse event could place an established hero beside a disabled version of themselves. A title might pass to a new person. A character might be reimagined after a major physical and emotional transformation.

The question should not be whether audiences could cope with it. Why wouldn't they? Superhero audiences already understand branching timelines, variants, resurrections, body swaps and characters who meet other versions of themselves. They can certainly understand that a familiar character now has a different body.

The more interesting question is what a disabled actor might discover in the character that the previous version could not.

## Difference and otherness are already part of the mythology

The creative possibilities are particularly rich within the X-Men, where physical difference is often inseparable from the story. Mutants are feared, stared at, excluded and categorised according to bodies they may not be able to conceal. Some can pass as non-mutant. Others cannot. Some take pride in being visibly different. Others mask their identities for safety, employment or belonging.

These are amplified versions of experiences familiar to many disabled people.

A person with facial difference may understand what it is like to enter a room and feel other people looking before anyone speaks. A wheelchair user may recognise the experience of a stranger addressing the person standing beside them instead. Someone with a limb difference or cerebral palsy may know when curiosity has become staring. A neurodivergent person may understand the continual calculation involved in deciding whether to mask.

This does not mean that every mutant should be assigned a disability diagnosis. It means disability lived experience can offer writers, directors and performers an interesting way into the material.

Nightcrawler's relationship with being stared at could be informed by people with visible difference. Rogue's physical boundaries could be explored alongside people who manage touch, immune vulnerability, chronic pain or sensory overload. Cyclops's reliance on his visor could be shaped by people whose access and safety depend upon technology that cannot be allowed to fail.

The value lies in the questions lived experience introduces.

How does someone know when they are being watched?  
When do they challenge it?  
Can visibility become a form of control?  
What is the emotional cost of passing?  
Does a device feel like part of the person, or something they are continually required to explain?

These questions are already present beneath the surface of many superhero stories. Disability does not have to be added to the metaphor. It can help a production understand what the metaphor has been all along.

## Disabled heroes, disabled villains and everyone between

There is no reason disabled casting should be restricted to admirable characters. A disabled performer can play a hero, villain, lover, victim, bureaucrat, coward, genius, criminal or fool. Disabled people are not a moral category.

The important creative distinction is between a villain who happens to be disabled and a story that uses disability as proof of villainy.

A disabled antagonist may understand exclusion perfectly and still make terrible choices. They may recognise other people's assumptions and use them strategically. They may build a community that offers belonging while demanding obedience. They may reject one oppressive system and then create another.

That complexity is valuable.

The multiverse makes room for this range because no single disabled character has to carry the entire weight of representation. One character does not have to be heroic, inspirational or morally reassuring when disabled people are present throughout the world. **The answer to limited representation is not one perfect disabled superhero, it is abundance.**

A blind hero, a wheelchair-using villain, an amputee journalist, a Deaf scientist, a neurodivergent monarch and a disabled person standing silently in the background can all exist in the same story. The more disabled people are present, the less any one of them has to stand for everyone else.

That is what a universe, and more importantly a multiverse, should feel like.

Then there are zombies.

Marvel and DC have both imagined worlds in which superheroes, villains and supporting characters become undead. Marvel's zombie universes have transformed almost every imaginable character, while DC has its own long tradition of undead figures, from Solomon Grundy to apocalyptic alternate worlds. A zombified body is unstable by definition. Cognition changes. Speech changes. Movement becomes irregular. Limbs may be missing or damaged. Bodies are reconstructed using practical effects, prosthetics and digital alteration.

This creates an obvious opportunity for physically disabled actors, amputee performers and disabled movement specialists.

An actor with limb difference may allow effects teams to create an undead body practically rather than digitally removing a limb from a non-disabled performer. Someone with a distinctive movement vocabulary may produce something more compelling and more convincing than an actor being instructed to stagger.

The horror must belong to the fictional transformation rather than to the performer's disability. But that is a matter of framing, writing and direction, not a reason to exclude disabled actors. Productions frequently spend significant amounts of money manufacturing physical difference while overlooking performers whose bodies could make the work more practical, original and convincing.

The multiverse asks audiences to accept a zombie Captain America, a cannibalistic Spider-Man or an undead version of almost any hero ever created.

It should not struggle to accept a disabled actor beneath the costume.

## The story behind the story

There is also a significant publicity opportunity, although publicity should never be the only reason for making a casting decision.

Audiences are interested in how familiar characters are reinvented. They want to understand why a new performer was chosen, how that actor connects with the character, what their own life experience is, how an action sequences were reimagined and how costume or technology responded to the actor.

A disabled performer entering an iconic role creates a story behind the story. And it is interesting.

What did the actor recognise in the character?  
How did lived experience affect the performance?  
What assumptions did the director reconsider?  
How did costume, prosthetics or production design change?  
What became possible because this actor was there?

These are stories about craft. They offer a production something more substantial than announcing that it has achieved representation. They demonstrate that the casting choice affected the work itself.

Sun-Spider is not interesting simply because she has Ehlers-Danlos syndrome. She is interesting because crutches fitted with web-shooters change what a Spider-person can look like and how that character can operate.

Makkari is not interesting simply because Lauren Ridloff is Deaf. Ridloff's casting created a different relationship between speed, sound, communication and a group of immortal beings.

A disabled Batman, Spider-Man or Wonder Woman should be approached in the same way. The question is not how disability can be accommodated within an established production. It is what the production can become because this performer is part of it.

## Anything is possible — so who is being imagined?

The multiverse is often described as a place where anything is possible.

That phrase should mean something.

If anything is possible, disability cannot remain confined to a small collection of established disabled characters. It cannot appear only as tragedy, injury or transformation. It cannot be something non-disabled actors are repeatedly invited to perform while disabled actors wait for the rare role that precisely matches their bodies.

In a multiverse, every character has innumerable disabled selves. Every hero has versions who move, sense, communicate and think differently.

Every villain does too.

So does every friend, lover, scientist, soldier, victim, ruler and person standing in the background.

Not every multiverse story must centre disability, and not every version of every character needs to be disabled. But when hundreds of variants are created and disability remains almost entirely absent, that absence becomes a creative choice. The multiverse removes the excuse that a disabled actor would be too different from the established character. Difference is the engine of the idea. It removes the excuse that changing the body would undermine authenticity. These stories alter bodies, histories and identities constantly. It removes the excuse that audiences would not understand. Audiences are already following parallel realities, branching timelines and characters who meet themselves. The remaining question is whether the people creating these cinematic worlds are prepared to imagine disabled people inside them?

Marvel and DC have developed a storytelling device capable of opening almost every role to almost any performer.

That is a remarkable creative opportunity.

A multiverse in which anything can happen, but disabled people still rarely appear, is not limited by its own rules. It is limited only by the imagination of those deciding who gets to exist.
